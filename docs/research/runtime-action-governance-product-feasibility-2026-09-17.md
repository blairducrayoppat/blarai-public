## The decisions that come back to the Lead Architect

*Six decisions are yours. Each carries a recommendation, the alternatives, and the trade-off. Nothing has been started, applied for, posted, purchased or registered; every one of these is still open.*

**1. Go or no-go, and whether the first year is a product, a specification, or both. [PROPOSED] Go, and make year one a specification plus one open reference implementation, not a paid product.** The alternatives are a full product now, or no-go. The trade-off: a paid product needs trust the founder cannot buy in year one, a company, a certificate, a support load and roughly the whole of his spare attention, and it would be aimed at a gap the platform vendor can close whenever it chooses. A specification with a working reference costs hundreds of euros, is the one asset that survives being copied, and answers the objection the target community actually voiced, which is not price but a closed privileged program from an unknown author. No-go is defensible only if you would not act on a good answer, because the technical claim came back proven: a governor can see and stop real agent actions on this machine today. Full reasoning in Q8.

**2. Open, commercial, or open-core. [PROPOSED] Open core under a genuine open-source licence, with any future paid tier in a separate directory under a licence that converts to open after two years.** The alternatives are the BlarAI structure (noncommercial plus a commercial licence), or fully proprietary. Three reasons, and the third only emerged from the law strand. The BlarAI structure keeps every right and every euro and closes three doors this study exists to open, because no foundation hosts a non-free licence, no platform vendor adopts a specification whose only implementation is noncommercial, and no one can legally continue the work if you stop. Open core forfeits exclusivity on the core, which the field's own history says was never protection anyway. And **BlarAI's licence would be the worst of both worlds for this product in the European Union**: because it forbids commercial use it does not meet either the resilience act's or the liability directive's definition of free and open-source, so publishing the governor under it would give away the source and keep every manufacturer duty. That is not a preference any more; it is a cost. A two-year conversion on the paid tier is what stops your disappearance from leaving users stranded.

**3. A name. [PROPOSED] none chosen; candidates offered, none cleared.** The study did no trademark search and made no registration. Descriptive directions that fit what the thing is: a word for a doorman or a gatekeeper, a word for a receipt or a ledger, or a plain compound naming the act (agent permissions, action governor). Whatever you pick needs a clearance search before it appears anywhere public, because the name is on the binary, the certificate and the store listing, and changing it later is expensive. This is the one decision with no technical content and the most consequence for the others.

**4. Whether to open a separate repository, and its rulebook. [PROPOSED] Yes, a separate repository from the first line, with its own decision records numbered from one.** The alternative is building it inside BlarAI and splitting later, which the study advises against: the two have different threat models, different users and different rules, and a split after the fact is where secrets and identity strings leak. What the new repository should inherit from BlarAI is its habits rather than its rules: a claim register, a coverage disclosure, the discipline that a finding is fixed or carries a checkable reason, a standing test gate where every control ships with a test proving it blocks and a test proving it can be switched off. What it must not inherit is the air-gap rulebook, the model-integrity machinery, the virtual machine, and the ceremonies that only make sense with one operator at one console. The full list is in Q7.

**5. Whether and how to approach Microsoft or a foundation, and when. [PROPOSED] Neither yet, and the order matters: contribute the vocabulary into the existing vendor-neutral standard in year one, approach a foundation only when a second independent implementation exists, and approach Microsoft not at all until there is something public to point at.** The alternatives are an early approach to Microsoft, or founding a rival standard. The trade-off: every foundation this study read wants what a solo project does not yet have, which is another maintainer or another implementer, and the one precedent of a solo founder taking a good design to Microsoft early ended with the design shipped by Microsoft and the founder retiring his tool. There is one new and concrete target for a proposal, found on this machine rather than in any document: Windows's agent protocol layer already has a filter slot that nothing this study read documents, with Microsoft's own security product as its only registrant on this machine, and Microsoft has separately published a third-party provider interface for a different feature that is exactly the right shape to propose for it.

**6. The legal form and jurisdiction, and the multi-user consent posture. [PROPOSED] No entity in year one if you take the specification path; a company before the first euro of revenue, in whichever member state you would actually deal with. For shared machines: per-user profiles and records, one machine-wide enforcement service, and an administrator-set floor a user may tighten but not loosen.** On the entity: an entity is required before any paid sale, it is what shields you from the liability the new product-liability rules make undisclaimable, and Microsoft's cheap signing identity is open to European companies and closed to European individuals, so a sole trader pays more for a certificate than a company does. None of that binds if year one has no revenue. On shared machines the trade-off goes on the box rather than in a policy: the machine's owner cannot read other accounts' records, which is the price of the product not being spyware. The member state remains yours to choose, and the formation examples in Q9 are illustrations rather than a recommendation.

## Outcome first

**The product is technically feasible on Windows today, its protection is thinner than it looks, and the thing worth building first is not the product.**

Feasible, and proven rather than argued. Three ways of getting between an AI agent and the machine were driven end to end here and worked. Through an agent's own hook, a command was refused before it ran, and the refusal held even when the agent was started in the mode that skips all its own safety questions. Through a proxy in front of an agent's tools, a call was seen and refused before the tool received it, the governor's own decision taking about a tenth of a millisecond in the live run and about forty-six millionths of a second at the median of a thirty-call scripted harness. Through a proxy in front of a real local model, a real model asked to delete a real-looking file, and the governor removed that instruction from the stream so the agent never saw it. None of that is a mock-up; all of it ran on this machine on 17 September.

Thinner than it looks, in three specific ways. Every one of those seams is a courtesy: the agent's own program runs the governor and honours its answer, the governor's foothold is an entry in a file the user can edit, and on all three agents examined a governor that crashes is read as having no opinion, so the action proceeds. That last one was measured here on one agent and read in the shipped code or the vendor's own words on the other two, and one of those vendors says plainly that its hook is a guardrail and not an enforcement boundary. An agent's shell command arrives as one line of text, so a governor sees "run this" and not "this sends your file to the internet"; the study demonstrated exactly that by fetching a web page inside a command that passed as ordinary. And the one seam that looks like a real operating-system control rather than a courtesy, the firewall, was accepted by every screen Windows offers and then quietly not enforced, because the antivirus suite installed here owns that seam. Thirty-seven attempts that should have been blocked all succeeded. A product that assumed it held that seam would tell people they were protected while they were not, which makes detecting and disclosing it a requirement rather than a refinement.

The thing worth building first. Every seam a third party can reach is cooperative, the one that is not needs a company, a certificate and a kernel driver on the side of the platform that Microsoft is actively dismantling, and Microsoft already ships this exact mechanism to enterprises using the same agent hooks this study probed. So the durable advantage was never going to be owning the seam. It is the parts nobody ships for a person's own machine: one way of describing what an agent is about to do that works across every agent, a record of every decision that the person owns and can check, an honest screen saying which agents are really governed and which are not, and a written specification others can implement. Those cost hundreds of euros rather than tens of thousands, they answer the objection the intended users actually voice, which is not price but a closed privileged program from an unknown author, and a specification is the only asset that survives the likeliest outcome if this becomes visible, which precedent says is the platform vendor building it themselves.

One finding on this machine deserves its own line, because it came from reading the artefact rather than the documentation and it points the wrong way from the usual story. Windows's own agent layer already contains a filter slot, an ordinary extension contract that a program can register against to sit in the path of agent tool calls. No Microsoft page this study read mentions it, and no exhaustive documentation search was run, so the honest statement is that it is undocumented in what was read rather than undocumented absolutely. On this machine exactly one thing registers it, and that thing is Microsoft Defender's own filter. A third party cannot build on a contract it cannot find documented, so the practical answer today is still no. But the place where a third-party policy interface would live is not hypothetical any more, and that turns a vague proposal into a concrete one.

## How to read this study

**The framing bias, and the legend that corrects for it.** The landscape compilation of 2026-09-16 scored the field on five questions (action identity, approval binding, receipt, enforcement seam, local operation). Those questions sit close to the framing of the Canonical Action Verification and Attestation (CAVA) and Proof-Carrying Agent Actions (PCAA) papers, which the Lead Architect read before the rest of the field, so those papers and their peers score well on them by construction. This study does not inherit that lens. It adds a second axis that the five questions under-weight: **seam ownership and reach**, meaning who owns the place where an action can be stopped, how many machines that place exists on, and what the owner can do there that a third party cannot. Q1, Q2 and Q15 are scored on that axis. The vocabulary of the field is borrowed freely because it is becoming a commons (the CAVA core is Apache-2.0, the Agent Hooks contract is MIT with a Microsoft copyright line, the Agent Control Standard is at OWASP under Apache-2.0); the study bets on no company.

**Grades.** MEASURED: run on this machine (Windows 11 Pro 25H2, build 10.0.26200.9457, Intel Core Ultra 7 258V, a Trusted Platform Module (TPM) version 2.0 from STMicroelectronics, which is the security chip on the motherboard, Bitdefender Total Security 27.0.60 installed and active, Microsoft Defender Antivirus not running) on 2026-09-17, with the command, the tool version, the timestamp and the result recorded in the probe record. MEASURED-AGAINST-A-MOCK: the mechanism was run here, but against a stand-in because the real counterpart is not installed on this machine; the grade says so wherever it applies. REPORTED: read in documentation or source; the source and its date are cited (`ms.date` for Microsoft Learn pages), and vendor prose is discounted against what the artefact shows. GUESSED: an estimate with its reasoning shown; never a fact.

**Conditions that apply to every number.** The machine was never idle: two or three other Claude Code sessions, the probe agents themselves and Bitdefender ran throughout. Timing figures carry their run counts and spreads; where a difference sits inside the run-to-run spread the study says so and draws no conclusion from it.

**Seam verdicts.** For each seam: what it can SEE (the meaning of the action, or only bytes and destinations), what it can STOP (before execution, or only observe), what ESCAPES it, what it COSTS (privileges, drivers, signing, user consent, maintenance), and whether it WORKS HERE TODAY.

### Seam 1. The agents' own hook interfaces (MEASURED on Claude Code; REPORTED for Copilot CLI and Codex)

**What was run.** Claude Code 2.1.273 was launched non-interactively from a throwaway directory with a probe settings file that registered a PreToolUse hook (a Python script) and excluded the user's own settings, so only the probe hook ran. Ten runs between 02:55 and 02:59 EDT; every payload the hook received was logged verbatim; the raw run outputs are in appendix A1.

**What the hook sees.** One JSON document per tool call: the session id, the prompt id, the transcript path, the working directory, the permission mode, the effort level, the event name, the tool name, the full tool input and a per-call tool-use id. Not in the payload: the model's reasoning, the user's prompt, the tool's eventual output, any child process, any network destination beyond what the command text happens to contain.

**What it stops, measured.** A hook that returns a JSON deny, or exits with code 2, blocked the command in every run that asked it to, and the block held when Claude Code was launched with `--dangerously-skip-permissions` and with `--permission-mode bypassPermissions`. The model received the reason and reported it; the caller of `claude -p` received the denial in a `permission_denials` list. An allow passed the command through. The hook can also rewrite the call's input, ask, or defer (documented, not run).

**What escapes it, measured.** Three things, each observed rather than read. First, `--safe-mode` skipped the hook entirely and the denied command ran (run e1). Second, a hook that crashes (exit code 1) is treated as no opinion and the command runs (run d2): the seam is fail-open on governor failure by design, and only exit 2 or an explicit JSON deny blocks. Third, the hook sees one command string and nothing of what the string does: a Python one-liner that fetched `https://example.com` passed as an allowed `Bash` call and returned status 200 (run c1); nothing in the event said "network". Documented but not run: `--bare` skips hooks, `disableAllHooks` disables them, and `--setting-sources` can exclude the file carrying the hook. Only hooks delivered as managed settings survive those switches, and the managed file is editable by a local administrator, which this user is. No managed policy exists on this machine (checked read-only at the documented file and registry locations).

**What it costs.** One Python process per matched tool call. Measured directly with the real payload on stdin, 15 runs after 3 warm-ups:

| Form | Runs | Minimum | Median | Maximum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| JSON allow/deny on stdout | 15 | 75.5 ms | 86.4 ms | 104.0 ms |
| exit-code-2 form | 15 | 81.5 ms | 86.7 ms | 98.6 ms |
| Python interpreter start alone | 15 | 73.5 ms | 88.5 ms | 96.5 ms |

The hook's own work is not resolvable above interpreter start-up; the cost is the launch. End to end, five interleaved pairs of `claude -p` runs with and without the hook read 13.7 s versus 13.5 s at the median inside an 8 to 23 s spread, so the hook's contribution cannot be separated from model latency at that sample size, which is consistent with the direct figure and does not independently confirm it. A governor written as a long-lived process reached over a pipe would avoid the launch cost.

**A record already on this machine.** The user-level hook that blocks destructive git commands keeps no log of its own; whether it has ever fired is recoverable only from Claude Code's transcripts, where a read-only search found eight denials across five transcripts, all on 2026-09-01. A governor that wants an audit trail must write it itself; the seam offers none.

**GitHub Copilot CLI 1.0.3 (REPORTED from the shipped code and the docs; not runnable here).** The installed bundle carries a `preToolUse` hook with the same shape (JSON on stdin with session id, timestamp, working directory, tool name and raw argument string; `permissionDecision: "deny"` or `modifiedArgs` honoured). In the 1.0.3 code a hook that errors, times out or crashes is caught and logged and the call proceeds (fail-open); the current documentation says non-zero exits are fail-closed for `preToolUse`, so the artefact and the prose disagree and the installed build is older than the docs. Hooks load from user-writable JSON files under `.github/hooks/` or `~/.copilot/hooks/`. The account's Copilot policy refused a harmless `copilot -p` with "Access denied by policy settings", so no probe ran and no login was attempted. Copilot CLI is the one agent here with a documented admin policy directory on Windows (`C:\ProgramData\GitHub\Copilot\policy.d`, from GitHub's docs).

**OpenAI Codex CLI (REPORTED; not installed).** The same hook shape (`PreToolUse` with tool name, tool-use id, tool input, session id, working directory; exit 2 or JSON deny blocks; `updatedInput` rewrites), fail-open on error and timeout, and OpenAI's own words: "Treat tool hooks as a useful guardrail, not a complete enforcement boundary." Two OpenAI pages disagree on whether the hooks feature is on by default. An admin can pin `allow_managed_hooks_only` in `requirements.toml`.

**Microsoft Defender for Endpoint's AI agent runtime protection (REPORTED, ms.date 2026-09-16, Preview).** It consumes exactly these three hook points (user prompt, pre-tool, post-tool) from Claude Code, Codex CLI, Copilot CLI and the Copilot app, adds network inspection for agents without hooks, and blocks or audits per event under tamper protection. It needs Defender for Endpoint Plan 2, Microsoft 365 E5, Agent 365 or E7 and an onboarded device with Defender Antivirus active. On this machine Defender Antivirus is not running and the Sense service is stopped (measured), so the seam does not exist here; Bitdefender is the active antivirus.

**Verdict.** Seam 1 is the richest seam a third party can occupy today and the one Microsoft's own governor uses. It is cooperative in every case: the agent's own process runs the governor and honours the answer, the registration is a JSON entry in a user-writable file, the agent offers switches that skip it, and a governor that crashes is treated as silence. What it gives in return is precise: the exact tool and arguments before execution, an id per call, the session and working directory, and on Claude Code the operator's permission mode, so a governor knows when it is the only lock left.

### Seam 2. A local Model Context Protocol proxy (MEASURED)

**What was run.** A minimal protocol server exposing one tool, and a proxy that launches it as a child, relays the protocol both ways, logs every call and refuses any whose arguments carry a forbidden token. The proxy, not the server, was registered with Claude Code for a single run through a per-run configuration file. Two runs at 02:52 and 02:53 EDT.

**What happened.** The proxy saw the call before the server did, with the tool name and the arguments in the clear, plus the agent's own tool-use identifier carried in the call's metadata. On the deny run it refused and did not forward; Claude Code reported to its caller, verbatim, "error: denied by governor proxy: arguments contain forbidden token". On the allow run it forwarded and the tool answered normally. Relay overhead measured over 30 scripted calls: 24.6 microseconds minimum, 46.3 median, 77.9 maximum for the governor's own decision, inside a round trip of 219 to 562 microseconds. The cost of this seam is negligible next to anything else in the path.

**How a governor gets in, and what that means.** Claude Code's server definitions live in the user's own configuration file (one server defined there on this machine) and in per-project files; a governor inserts itself by rewriting a configuration entry to point at the proxy instead of the server. That is a configuration-level, cooperative seam: the same person or a malicious tool can point it back. For servers reached over HTTP rather than as child processes, a governor needs a URL rewrite or a local reverse proxy with its own certificate, which is a heavier and more intrusive shape.

**What escapes it.** Everything that is not a protocol tool call. Claude Code's own built-in tools, which are where the file writes and shell commands happen, do not pass through it; agents that do not speak the protocol do not either; and servers an agent launches for itself are outside any proxy the governor did not configure. On this machine a governor would need both seam 1 and seam 2 to see all of one agent's actions.

### Seam 3. Windows's own agent layer, on this build (MEASURED, and it corrects the documentation)

The documentation says this layer is Insider-only, at a build higher than this machine's. The registry and the file system say the plumbing is already here, and they say one thing the documentation does not.

**What is present on this Windows 11 Pro 25H2 machine, build 10.0.26200.9457, measured read-only.** A registry key naming the on-device registry's command (`odr.exe`, with the argument `mcp`), while the executable itself is absent from the system directory. An agent-governance configuration service provider, `AgentGovernanceCSP.dll`, "Agent Governance configuration service provider", version 10.0.26100.9278, last written 2026-09-08. A protocol provider library, `DAFMCP.dll`. Shell server packaging components and per-language server definition files for the File Explorer and Settings connectors. A `Windows.AI.Agents.dll` in the system directory and a `Windows.AI` metadata file. A dedicated service host group for protocol management. The full enterprise policy surface under the policy manager, on a Pro edition and a general-availability build: the connector access policy, the consent duration (default 720 hours, matching the published figure), the connector configuration, the on-device registry logging level, and the settings-agent switch. Also present, and relevant: the Recall data-loss-prevention provider policy, the one documented third-party policy hook in this stack.

**The finding that matters.** Windows's application-extension catalogue on this machine registers a contract named `com.microsoft.windows.ai.mcpFilter`, alongside the documented `com.microsoft.windows.ai.mcpServer`. Exactly one package registers the filter contract, and the staged package is `MdOdrMcpFilterPackage`, published under Microsoft's own publisher identity; the binary behind it is `MdOdrMcpFilter.exe` inside the Microsoft Defender platform directory, whose file description reads "Microsoft Defender MCP Message Filter COM Server", version 4.18.26060.3008. In other words: **a filter slot in Windows's agent protocol layer exists, it is an ordinary packaged application-extension contract, and Microsoft Defender occupies it.** No Microsoft documentation read by this study mentions the filter contract at all; the published extension point for third parties is the server contract only.

That changes the deep-integration answer in a way documentation alone could not. It is no longer "no slot exists"; it is "a slot exists, nothing this study read documents it, and its only registrant here is the platform vendor's own security product". Two things follow. A third party cannot rely on it: an undocumented contract can change or be locked to Microsoft-signed packages at any time, and nothing here establishes that a non-Microsoft package registering that contract would be loaded, which this study did not attempt and could not attempt without installing a package. And a specification proposal now has a concrete target: the filter contract is the place a policy-provider interface would live, and the Recall provider interface, which Microsoft did document for third parties, is the shape it should take.

**What a third party can do with this layer today: nothing as a governor.** It can be a server (governed by the layer) or a host (governing only its own agent). The layer does not sit on the path of Claude Code or Codex, which reach their own servers directly.

### Seam 4. Agent accounts and the agent workspace (MEASURED)

No agent accounts exist on this machine. The local accounts are the operator's own, two custom local accounts, a restricted account this project created for its coder, and two accounts named for Codex's offline and online sandboxes. That last pair is itself evidence for seam 7: OpenAI's Windows sandbox creates dedicated low-privilege local accounts, which is an agent vendor doing operating-system isolation for itself, without a governor.

**Correction, found by the independent review and verified before this document merged.** An earlier draft of this section stated that no agent or Copilot application packages were installed for any user. That was false, and the way it became false is the thing worth recording: the command behind it was run in a non-elevated shell with errors suppressed, so it threw "Access is denied" and returned nothing, and the nothing was read as an absence. Re-run correctly, three packages are installed for the current user: the Copilot application at version 153.0.4234.42, the Windows client artificial-intelligence system component, and an inference-agent toolkit package. The retracted sentence is left visible here rather than quietly deleted, because one section earlier this same study warns that an absence reported by a broken command is not an absence, and the warning did not save the author from it.

What survives the correction: no agent *accounts* exist, which is the separate finding and rests on a command that returned real output. The Copilot runtime is switched off in the current user's registry. A third-party governor gains nothing from this layer today, and nothing this study read documents third-party enrolment or scope observation if it ships broadly.

### Seam 5. A local model-server proxy (MEASURED against a real server and a mock)

**The strongest stop seam found outside the agents' own hooks, and it was proven against real software.** A governor proxy was placed in front of `llama-server` from llama.cpp, running Qwen3-0.6B on the processor. Both the server binary and the model file pre-date this study by two months (written 2026-07-10 and 2026-07-16, in the operator's own model-comparison directory), so nothing was installed for the probe; the server was started for the probe and stopped afterwards.

**What happened.** Asked to delete a file, the real local model emitted a real tool call for `delete_file` with the path as its argument. Straight from the server, the client received that tool call with a finish reason of "tool calls", which is exactly the moment an agent would execute it. Through the governor, the client received a finish reason of "stop" and the text "Refused by governor: delete_file is not permitted by profile", and no tool call at all. The proxy saw the intent in the stream before the agent did, in both the streamed and the non-streamed forms, and the agent could not act on what it never received. On the control case, where the request was permitted, the governor changed nothing.

**What it costs, including the measurement that argues against the seam.** Against a mock server with 200-token responses over three runs, all three comparisons, because two of them are less flattering than the one an earlier draft reported:

| Path | Direct | Through the governor |
|---|---|---|
| Streamed, time to first token | 36.8 ms | 48.6 ms |
| Streamed, total | 40.1 ms | 58.3 ms |
| Non-streamed, total | 17.9 ms | 63.8 ms |

Against the real local server, five runs each, the proxied path read faster than the direct path at the median, 169 ms against 256 ms to first token and 7.3 s against 13.0 s in total. That is not a claim that a proxy accelerates anything: generation time dominates by two orders of magnitude and varied by a factor of two between runs, so the proxy's contribution is not resolvable at that sample size on a machine running several other agents.

**Two honest readings, and they point different ways.** Against a real local model the added cost is invisible, because the model is thousands of times slower than the proxy. Against a mock that answers instantly, the added cost is 12 to 46 milliseconds, which is two to nine times the five-millisecond budget Q12 proposes. The reconciliation is that the mock figures measure this probe's implementation, a Python relay with per-request connection setup, and not the seam itself: the same strand's separate measurement of a resident governor reached over a named pipe is 29 to 34 microseconds. So the seam is cheap and **this probe's proxy is not**, and a shipped implementation must be a resident process rather than a per-request relay to stay inside the budget. That distinction was blurred in an earlier draft, which reported only the flattering comparison and described the proxy's internal work as though it were the whole cost.

**What escapes it.** Agents talking to cloud models over transport encryption the product must not intercept, which is most agents most of the time; tool execution that happens on the server side rather than in the agent; and the vocabulary problem, since seeing a tool call named `delete_file` is not the same as knowing what it will delete. A governor inserts itself here by rewriting one environment variable or configuration key, which is cooperative and reversible by the user.

### Seam 6. The network layer (MEASURED, and it failed)

**The per-process outbound block did not work on this machine, and it failed silently.** A Windows Firewall rule blocking outbound traffic for one copy of `curl` was accepted by every management surface: the cmdlet reported success, the rule listing showed it, the registry held it, and the firewall event log recorded the addition. Then all 37 requests that program made while the rule was in place returned HTTP 200, exactly as the five baseline requests had. The rule was never turned into a kernel filter: a dump of the filtering platform taken while the rule was in place contains zero filters for it, searched by name and by identifier across all 2,405 filters. The same is true of a pre-existing rule this project created for its own coder.

**Why.** Bitdefender is registered with the Windows firewall service as a third-party firewall product claiming the boot, stealth and firewall rule categories, which is the documented mechanism by which a third-party firewall "takes ownership from Windows Firewall". In the kernel, Microsoft's own firewall provider holds 18 filters, all of them application-container loopback and boot-time entries; Bitdefender's two drivers hold 2,176 filters and 44 callouts, and its sublayer sits at the top of the arbitration order. The only visible symptom in the normal management surface is a line reading that local firewall rules are not applicable. Nothing says "your rules are not being enforced".

**What this means for the product, and it is a design requirement, not a detail.** A consumer governor cannot assume it owns the network seam, because any security suite with a firewall takes it, and the failure is silent. A governor using this path must first enumerate the registered firewall products and treat a non-empty list as "this seam is not mine", then say so on the coverage screen. The Security Centre is not a reliable substitute for that check: on this machine it still reports a McAfee firewall and antivirus as enabled whose program directory no longer exists. To own the seam regardless of the installed suite, a governor would have to be its own filtering-platform provider in its own sublayer, which this study did not probe and which would still be arbitrated against Bitdefender's top-weight sublayer.

**What the seam could see at best.** Program path, user identity, address, port, protocol, interface and profile. Nothing semantic: not the host name, not the address requested, not the method, not the content. Anything semantic needs a kernel callout driver, which is tier (b) of Q2. Name resolution is filtered per domain, not per application, and is empty here. Transport encryption is out of bounds for a consumer governor; reading inside it needs a trusted root certificate, and Bitdefender has installed one on this machine, though the one encrypted connection tested was not intercepted.

### Seam 7. Isolation: AppContainer, restricted tokens, Windows Sandbox, application control (MEASURED)

**AppContainer works from user mode with nothing installed.** A 200-line Python script created an AppContainer profile, launched probe executables inside it, and the operating system denied the container's write to the scratch directory, its write to `C:\Windows\Temp`, and its read of the user's git configuration; the container could write only to its own package folder. With no capabilities the container could not resolve a hostname (`curl` error 6); with the `internetClient` capability it fetched `https://example.com` and got 200. The profile was deleted cleanly and nothing remained. The launcher's own token opened the image, which is why an executable the container itself could not read still ran; children of the container were not tested (the probe failed on a working-directory error before it measured anything).

**A restricted token removes admin and nothing else.** `runas /trustlevel:0x20000` from the elevated shell produced a process with the Administrators group marked deny-only and a single privilege left, which could not write to `C:\Program Files` or `C:\Windows` but wrote freely to `C:\Windows\Temp` and the user's folders. The integrity label stayed High because the parent was elevated.

**Application control decides whether code may run, never what it may touch.** Measured state: Windows Defender Application Control enforces four Microsoft policies on this consumer Pro machine and `CiTool` answers, so a third party could deploy a supplemental policy without any device-management enrolment; AppLocker has zero rules and its service is stopped; Smart App Control is off (registry state 0) and has no third-party surface at all. Windows Sandbox is disabled and enabling it needs a restart, so no sandbox was run; the study rules forbade the change. Hyper-V is present with BlarAI's own virtual machine, listed and not touched. The signer rules in application control do not match ECDSA-signed files (REPORTED, ms.date 2026-03-29).

**The cost is the point.** For an agent the person did not write and does not trust, the container is right: the operating system, not the agent, enforces the boundary. For the developer's own coding agent the same denials are the failure mode: it lost the person's files, the credentials in the profile, every installed tool outside `System32`, and the network until a capability was granted. Brokering those back in one at a time is a product in itself, and nothing in the operating system brokers per action: the difference between `git status` and `git push` does not exist below the process boundary. The two coding agents on this machine are unpackaged Win32 executables (`claude.exe` under the user profile, `copilot.exe` under the winget packages folder), so Microsoft's Win32 app isolation, which needs the program repackaged in Windows's modern packaging format (MSIX), does not apply to them as they ship.

### Seam 8. Observation: Event Tracing for Windows and the Event Log (MEASURED)

**What an admin-started trace saw, without a kernel driver.** Three inbox kernel providers were enabled one at a time on a throwaway session and then together. The process provider gave a process-start receipt with process id, parent id, session, integrity label, elevation and the full image path, and no command line (the version 4 event has no such field). The network provider gave, for a probe copy of `curl`, a connection-attempt event with process id, local and remote address and port, and no hostname, no URL and no content. The file provider gave a create event with the full path and the create options. Every receipt arrives after the fact; none can stop anything.

**What it costs.** Two things the product must design around. First, volume: the three providers together wrote 290 MB in 60 seconds at the keywords chosen (the machine was under the load described above; file growth was measured every five seconds and is the trustworthy figure; the CPU delta is confounded by the concurrent agents and is reported only as a direction). A shipped product must filter hard, by process id and keyword, with circular buffers. Second, privilege: a member of Performance Log Users could create the session but enabling any of the three kernel providers was denied, and the registry security descriptors confirm why (they grant enable rights only to SYSTEM, LOCAL SERVICE and Administrators). A governor observing through these providers needs a service running as LOCAL SERVICE or SYSTEM, installed once by an administrator. The Security event log offered nothing: process-creation auditing is off, command-line logging is off, Sysmon is not installed, and the 52 process-creation events present are boot-time artefacts. The EDR-grade provider (Threat-Intelligence) needs an anti-malware protected process backed by a Microsoft-signed early-launch driver, which only Microsoft Virus Initiative members can obtain (REPORTED; Microsoft Learn documents the protected-process mechanism, dated 2018-08-02 and 2024-08-19, and the provider linkage is community-attested).

**Verdict.** Observation is real, cheap to reach as administrator, and useless for stopping anything. It is the receipt seam for agents no other seam covers, and its receipts carry process identity and the resource touched, never the command line, the hostname or the meaning.

### Seam 9. Identity and keys: Windows Hello and the Trusted Platform Module (MEASURED)

**Presence.** The Windows Hello availability check returned Available, with a face camera and a fingerprint sensor present; no verification prompt was raised. BlarAI's presence verifier reaches the same Windows API through a small C# helper and treats every outcome other than a clear yes as no; it reads no configuration and hard-codes no machine facts, so it runs on any Windows 10 or 11 PC where Hello is enrolled for the user, with the helper built and signed by the product (REPORTED from source).

**Keys.** The TPM is present and ready (specification 2.0, firmware 9.256.0.0) and the Microsoft Platform Crypto Provider is listed. BlarAI's signer uses only that provider through the public Windows key-storage interface, Cryptography API: Next Generation (CNG), creates non-exportable elliptic-curve keys that cannot be copied off the chip in the per-user key store, and hard-codes no key names, so the same code runs on any Windows 11 PC with a TPM 2.0. A throwaway key was created, exercised and deleted twice; the four pre-existing BlarAI keys were listed before and after and were unchanged.

**The number that shapes the receipt design.** With the key handle held open, a TPM signature took 77 to 94 ms at the median across two runs of 30; opened per call, as BlarAI's signer does today, 131 to 157 ms. A software ECDSA signature took 0.027 ms (500 runs). The chip executes one command at a time, so it delivers roughly ten to twelve signatures per second at full occupancy. A coding agent emitting tens of tool calls a minute could be signed per action; a governor serving several agents, or signing every file and network receipt from seam 8, cannot use the TPM per event. The workable shape is a software key sealed by the TPM that signs every receipt in microseconds, with the TPM signing the hash-chain head on a timer or per batch. This is the basis of the performance budget in Q12.

## Q2. The deep-integration path: where a governor could live so that agents cannot go around it

**The documentation's answer, and the machine's correction.** Read on its own, the documentation says a third party cannot be the authorising component in Windows's agent protocol layer, because no policy-provider interface is published. The machine says something more precise and more useful: a filter slot exists, absent from every Microsoft page this study read, as an ordinary packaged application-extension contract named `com.microsoft.windows.ai.mcpFilter`, and its only registrant on this machine is Microsoft Defender's own protocol message filter (seam 3, MEASURED). The practical answer for a third party is the same today, because a contract with no published interface cannot be built on and nothing establishes that a non-Microsoft package would be loaded into it. The strategic answer is different: the place a policy-provider interface would live already exists, which makes a specification proposal concrete rather than speculative. This is the study's clearest instance of the project's own rule that documentation is not evidence, and it runs in the direction that makes the platform look more open than its documentation, not less.

**Headline, REPORTED from ten Microsoft Learn pages and two Windows Developer Blog posts.** A third party cannot be the authorising component in Windows's own Model Context Protocol layer today. The on-device agent registry and its MCP proxy do authentication, authorisation and auditing themselves (Windows Developer Blog, 2025-11-18); across the MCP overview (ms.date 2025-12-06, updated 2026-06-04), the containment page (2025-11-11), the server-registration pages (2025-08-12 and 2025-11-10), the host quickstart (2025-08-12), the `odr.exe` tool page (2025-10-31), the WindowsAI policy CSP (2026-06-22, updated 2026-08-18) and the Build 2026 posts (2026-06-02) there is no policy-provider interface, no third-party authoriser slot, no documented event channel for the proxy, and no consumer-editable policy surface. A third party can be a server (an "agent connector", registered through an MSIX extension with static tool listings) or a host (an app that shells `odr.exe`). It cannot be the thing that says yes or no. The layer is Insider-only (every page names build 26220.7262 or higher as the prerequisite; this machine is 26200), the enterprise policies for it are marked Enterprise and Education only, not Pro, and no page announces a general-availability date. The JSON schema for the access policy is advertised at a link that redirected back to the policy page on 2026-09-17.

**Microsoft's own agent governor uses no deep seam.** Defender for Endpoint's AI agent runtime protection sits on the agents' vendor hooks (seam 1) plus network inspection, the same places a third party can occupy. Its advantages are tamper protection tied to Defender Antivirus, the incident console, and the licence, not a private operating-system hook.

**The one documented third-party policy hook in the Windows AI stack** is the Recall Data Loss Prevention Provider API (ms.date 2025-12-01): an Authenticode-signed DLL loaded in-process, returning Allow, AuditAndAllow, Warn or Block per capture, selected by one Group Policy string, Enterprise and Education only. It is the existence proof that Microsoft will adopt a provider-DLL contract for a policy decision, and the shape a specification proposal for the MCP proxy should copy.

**The routes, ranked by the enforcement a third party can actually occupy today.**

**What only Microsoft can do, and what a third party can do today, in one sentence each.** Microsoft alone can put a governor where an agent cannot go around it without a driver, because it owns the proxy, the consent surface and the agent identity. A third party today can occupy every cooperative seam (hooks, launcher containment, firewall, application control, observation) and ship the policy, the vocabulary, the receipts and the honest coverage screen on top of them.

**The two places where a specification, not a product, is the only thing a third party can offer.** First, a policy-provider contract for the MCP proxy and the agent workspace, shaped like the Recall DLP Provider API: a signed component answering Allow, AuditAndAllow, Warn or Block per tool call with host identity, server identity, tool name, arguments and agent account as context. Nothing a third party ships can occupy that slot until Microsoft creates it; the only lever is a written contract Microsoft could adopt, carried under a vendor-neutral banner (the OWASP Agent Control Standard or the Agent Hooks contract) rather than a company. Second, a cross-vendor pre-action hook contract, the "AMSI for agent actions": Microsoft's own enterprise product already consumes one bespoke hook per vendor, so a published payload schema with block semantics and tamper-evidence expectations is the single place where a small actor's reach can equal a platform's, because the platform is already consuming the same seam.

**Distribution, from the same strand.** Microsoft Store registration is free for individuals and companies (ms.date 2025-09-17; blog 2026-05-07), a one-person company selling software must use a company account (policy 10.14), and Store policy 10.2.4 (version 7.20, ms.date 2026-09-14) makes a dependency on a non-Microsoft NT service or driver "generally not allowed", case by case for certified drivers, so a governor with a Windows service is at Microsoft's discretion in the Store and should ship its Store edition user-mode-only. winget is a free pull request whose pipeline runs several antivirus engines and refuses "genuine tools used for debugging and low-level activities" that read as potentially unwanted; a hooking governor is the shape it refuses until the engines clear it. Azure Artifact Signing (formerly Trusted Signing) costs about $9.99 a month for 5,000 signatures (Azure retail price list, effective 2024-06-01, read 2026-09-17), is open to organisations in the European Union with no minimum company age (Microsoft answer, 2026-08-17), and is closed to individuals outside the United States and Canada, so an EU individual needs a company or a certificate from a commercial authority (organisation-validated from about $110 to $315 a year; individual-validated from about $129 a year, both on hardware or a cloud signing service). No certificate buys SmartScreen reputation any more, Extended Validation included (Microsoft Learn, ms.date 2026-05-04); reputation takes "several weeks and hundreds of clean installs", and Artifact Signing customers lost it twice in 2026 through silent certificate-authority rotations (GitHub issues 128 and 136, April and May 2026). Not verified: official Artifact Signing prices on the public page (it shows placeholders), whether a Windows service inside an MSIX package passes Store certification, and the Windows edition gating of the consumer agent settings page.

## Q3. Universal profiles: one vocabulary, three profiles, and the device scan

**The vocabulary [PROPOSED], eleven fields.** Who acted (the agent, its version, the session). What kind of action it is, as a *set* rather than one value, because fetching a web page is both a send and a receive and installing something is three things at once: read, write, delete, execute, network-send, network-receive, credential-use, device-use, settings-change, purchase-or-payment, message-send, install, and unknown. What it touches (user documents, the project tree, the system, removable media, cloud-synced folders, the network, accounts, devices, other applications, unknown). Whether it can be undone (reversible, reversible with a backup, irreversible, unknown). Where the data goes (local, loopback, the local network, the internet, with the host). What class of data is involved (none, personal, credential, financial). The concrete target, with a display form and a hash. The raw tool name and a hash of the canonical arguments. And one field this study adds because nothing in the surveyed vocabularies carries it: **how the mapping was derived** — declared by the agent, parsed from its arguments, taken from a tool annotation, or unknown. Without that field a profile cannot tell a fact from a guess, and every honest thing the product says downstream depends on the difference. The action's identity is a hash over the first ten fields with the clock excluded, which is the choice BlarAI already made correctly.

**Relation to what exists.** Of BlarAI's canonical action fields, four survive with changes, one is dropped as a microservice concept the product has no use for, and one is replaced: a sensitivity grade becomes a data-class kind, because "how bad" is a profile's judgment while "what kind" is a fact. Six of the thirteen categories have no equivalent in BlarAI's verbs at all, and they are exactly the ones a home user cares about most: using a credential, using a device, changing a setting, spending money, sending a message, installing software. The vocabulary borrows freely and attributes: the canonical-action work whose core is Apache-2.0, the Agent Control Standard at OWASP whose code and schemas are Apache-2.0 and whose prose is Creative Commons, and the Agent Hooks contract under a permissive licence. Nothing is imported as a dependency, so a dead upstream costs one version string. The protocol's own tool annotations already default to the safe answer (assume destructive, assume open world) and the specification says clients must treat them as untrusted, so they enter this vocabulary as derived-from-annotation under a rule that they may only tighten a decision, never loosen one, which is the generalisation of the rule BlarAI already applies to model judgments.

**The weakest point in the whole design, stated plainly.** A shell command is one opaque string. The measured example from this machine is the proof: a command that fetched a web page passed as an ordinary allowed shell call, with no network category, no host and nothing else to see. So a shell tool is honestly "execute, unknown scope, derivation unknown". A parser can only tighten, and must refuse outright the case it cannot read, which is an interpreter invoked with inline code, because that is precisely the measured example and the interpreter is an ordinary permitted program. Even a perfect parser has a lower ceiling than it looks: running a script by name is unknown to any parser, and the file can change between the check and the execution. **The answer is therefore not a better parser but a second independent lock on the process, and on this machine that lock is missing**, because the firewall seam is owned by the installed antivirus and the per-process block failed silently. That single fact ties the vocabulary, the developer profile and the coverage screen together, and it is the strongest argument in the study for shipping the coverage screen before shipping confidence.

**One agent mapped, and one gap that changes an adapter's design.** Claude Code's tools map cleanly except for the shell: reads and searches are read over a path class, edits and writes are write and reversible-with-backup inside a project tree, web fetching is network-receive to a host, and protocol tools carry their server's name. GitHub Copilot's command-line tool set was read from its shipped definition files, and it has **no per-call identifier and no permission mode in its hook payload**. That is not a detail: it means the only binding available on that agent is the argument hash re-derived immediately before execution, so what is a good practice elsewhere is mandatory there.

**A hole that was checked and is not there.** An agent that can dispatch subagents could, in principle, run tool calls that never reach the governor. Measured on this machine: a subagent's shell call fired the parent's hook, and a denial inside a subagent blocked the action and surfaced in the caller's result. The subagent tool is not a hole in this agent's coverage. It is a behaviour rather than a documented contract, so each adapter's conformance suite should pin it per agent version.

**The three profiles and the device scan [PROPOSED].** A profile is a file in a plain-key format that a person can read aloud, with the application showing a plain-language rendering beside it: a cautious home profile that denies the irreversible and anything touching credentials or money and asks about personal documents; a developer profile that allows reads and writes inside project trees, asks before deletes outside version control, denies credential use, leases network access to package registries, and always asks before a purchase or a message; and a small-business profile that adds retention, a mandatory weekly digest and per-machine copies. Every profile needs an explicit default for an agent it does not recognise, and that default is deny or ask, never allow. The device scan enumerates what agents could reach and never reads content or sends anything: user folders, cloud-synced folders, credential stores, network adapters and virtual private networks, cameras and microphones and removable drives, installed agents, local model runtimes, and the protocol server configurations found in the agents' own files. The scan on this machine found a settings folder for an agent that is not installed, which is the case the scan exists to surface: a configuration directory is a place a hook can be registered before the agent arrives.

## Q3a. The antivirus interaction model, and where it breaks

**What transfers.** The consumer security products converge on the same four devices, and each has an analogue here. Allow-once or allow-always becomes a lease, and gains the expiry that an exclusion list never has. Exclusions become per-agent, per-path rules, and the existing products already scope an exclusion to a single executable, which is the right grain. The periodic security report becomes the weekly digest. The activity feed becomes the receipt history. One smaller idea is worth stealing outright: the product that turns its tray icon a different colour while its quiet mode is on shows continuous, visible degradation, and that is exactly what the coverage screen's reduced state should look like.

**Where it breaks, and these are not refinements.** There is no quarantine for an action and no restore: a file the antivirus took away can be given back, and a payment that has been made cannot. "Reputation" has no local analogue, and the product must not invent one it cannot compute. A deferral mode that holds notifications during a game is right for alerts and wrong for approvals, so the design holds notifications and refuses the must-interrupt items rather than queuing them. And the telemetry posture is the decision that makes everything else believable: a governor that phones home cannot sell a local-only promise, so the default is none, opt-in diagnostics are inspectable as plain text before they are sent, and that is stated on the screen rather than in a policy document.

## Q4. The coverage screen

**Four states, with the honest one as the default.** Per installed agent: governed, and through which seam; observed only, and through which; out of reach; or unknown, which is what a newly seen agent gets until an adapter proves otherwise. The screen answers the six auditability questions per agent in one line each, and question three is where it earns its place, because for a typed shell command the honest answer on screen is that no rule about it is mechanically checkable. For agents it cannot govern the screen offers the real options rather than an apology: sandbox it, restrict its network, or accept the risk knowingly.

**Two behaviours that make it an instrument rather than a claim.** When an adapter's periodic canary call is not intercepted, that agent drops from governed to unknown, every lease bound to it is cancelled, and the person is told regardless of the noise setting; the gap is then bounded by time rather than hidden. And because of the measured firewall result, the screen carries a row for a seam this product does not own, driven by enumerating the registered firewall products: when another security product owns the network seam, the screen says so in plain words instead of implying a protection that the measurement showed is not there.

## Q5. The commercial path

**Who is there to sell to (REPORTED unless marked).** Windows 11 runs 68.6% of desktop Windows worldwide and 71.3% in Europe (StatCounter, August 2026); Microsoft's own top-line figures are 1.6 billion monthly active Windows devices (earnings call, 2026-04-30, secondary reporting) and 1 billion on Windows 11 (2026-01-28, secondary). No source gives an absolute EU count; a GUESSED derivation from EU population and a PC density of 0.35 to 0.45 per head puts 75 to 100 million Windows 11 PCs in the EU-27. The developer segment is measured in the low millions per tool: Codex above 5 million weekly users (OpenAI, June 2026), Claude Code used by 39% of professional developers globally in a JetBrains survey of more than 15,000 (August 2026), GitHub Copilot 4.7 million paid subscribers (Microsoft, 2026-01-28); roughly half of professional developers run Windows (Stack Overflow 2025) and no source splits agent users by operating system, so the Windows share of command-line agent users is GUESSED at 35 to 45%. EU micro and small enterprises number 33.2 million (Eurostat, 2025-12-09); their PC count is GUESSED at 63 to 84 million and overlaps the consumer segment because most micro enterprises are sole traders on consumer PCs.

**What people pay, and for what (REPORTED, vendor pages fetched 2026-09-17).** Consumer security suites sell at 40 to 60 dollars or euros for the first year across three to five devices and renew at two to three times that: Bitdefender Total Security $59.99 or €49.99 first year; Norton 360 Deluxe $49.99 renewing at $124.99 (€39.99 renewing at €109.99); ESET €49.99 to €119.99. Agent-security vendors either give the core away and charge for coordination (Pipelock: free community edition, $99 a month Pro, $999 a year, $25,000 a year enterprise; its founding-price counter read "2 of 50 claimed" on 2026-09-17) or sell to enterprises with no public price (Lasso, Zenity, Noma, Runlayer, HiddenLayer, Straiker). Trust products people do pay for, with recent price rises and no visible churn data: 1Password (Individual $47.88 a year after a 33% rise on 2026-03-27), Bitwarden (Premium $19.80 a year, doubled in January 2026).

**The reading of those numbers.** Consumers pay for security in bundles at $40 to $60 a year and rarely for a single utility; developers pay for tools that make them faster and have not yet been shown paying for a guard (the one live data point, Pipelock's founding counter, is tiny); the only per-app governor with a twenty-year one-time-price record is on the other operating system. A governor's plausible price shapes, by segment, are set out in the business-shape table once the antivirus-market half of this question returns; the evidence so far supports a free open core with a paid tier for the developer and small-business segments, and no evidence yet supports a consumer subscription on its own.

**Competition, and who owns the seams (REPORTED).** Microsoft already owns and uses both seams the product would sit on, for enterprises only. Defender for Endpoint's AI agent runtime protection (public preview since June 2026; ms.date 2026-09-16) is the mechanism of the hypothesis built by the platform vendor: hooks into Claude Code, Codex CLI, Copilot CLI and the Copilot app, block mode, a toast on the desktop, tamper protection, discovery of roughly thirty agent types and their MCP configurations (ms.date 2026-05-27), all gated behind Defender for Endpoint Plan 2, Microsoft 365 E5, Agent 365 (US$15 per user per month, generally available 2026-05-01) or E7. Windows itself has a consumer-visible per-agent permission page (Settings, System, AI components, Agents, per agent, Files: Allow always, Ask every time, Never; support article updated 2025-12-05, builds 26100.7344 and later) but it governs only agents inside Microsoft's agent workspace and registry model, today Copilot Actions and MCP connectors, not Claude Code or Codex running as ordinary processes. The funded start-up field is almost entirely hosted enterprise control planes (Zenity $125 million, 2026-08-03; Noma $100 million, 2025-07-31; HiddenLayer $100 million, 2026-09-02; Runlayer $42 million total; acquisitions of Prompt Security by SentinelOne, Lakera by Check Point, CalypsoAI by F5, Aim by Cato, Acuvity by Proofpoint, Invariant by Snyk, Protect AI by Palo Alto); the endpoint vendors (CrowdStrike, SentinelOne, Zscaler, Cisco) are adding agent discovery and prompt-layer protection to their sensors; 1Password and Endor Labs govern coding agents for businesses through hooks. **Founder credibility, and an asymmetry worth acting on.** The certification and the upstream contributions read almost inversely to the segments. To a cautious home user, both are invisible. The recorded objection in the communities that would adopt this is not to unqualified founders; it is to closed source and to privileged code written by artificial intelligence. ## Q6. The open-source path

**The licences the field actually uses (REPORTED from each repository's licence file, read through the GitHub API on 2026-09-17 with the blob hash recorded in appendix B3).** Every specification-shaped artefact is permissive: the CAVA core is Apache-2.0; the Agent Hooks contract is MIT with a Microsoft Corporation copyright line, in an organisation Microsoft describes as open but whose governance file names one Microsoft-employed maintainer; the Agent Control Standard at OWASP is Apache-2.0 for code and CC BY-SA 4.0 for prose, with a governance file, a security policy and named workstream leads; Microsoft Agent Framework and the Agent Governance Toolkit are MIT; the Model Context Protocol's specification repository carries a formal transition notice from MIT to Apache-2.0. The two products that sell something split by directory, not by licence of the whole: Pipelock is Apache-2.0 with an Elastic License 2.0 `enterprise/` directory, Mastra is Apache-2.0 outside an `ee/` directory under its own proprietary enterprise licence. Copyleft appears only on the two end-user firewalls (Portmaster and LuLu, both GPL-3.0); no adoption-seeking specification in this field is copyleft. One correction to the brief's premise: nothing read supports a wind-down of the open-source network governor the brief named; it shipped a release in August 2026 and cut its paid-tier price in February 2026. Its own about page now says it is developed and operated by a virtual-private-network company, so the project changed operator rather than ending, which is itself a continuity precedent worth noting.

**BlarAI's licence is no obstacle, and the reverse direction is the real decision.** BlarAI is under PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0 with a commercial licence held by the sole author. `git shortlog` over all branches lists one natural-person author (4,088 commits under three spellings of the same name and address) plus eleven commits under this machine's own agent identities; there is no contributor of record, no contributor agreement and no third-party copyright header in the runtime tree. PolyForm binds licensees, not the licensor, and says in terms that it does not prevent the licensor from granting other licences, so the author can publish any of his own code under Apache-2.0 or MIT by choosing to; the mechanics are a new licence header and a NOTICE file. The premise this rests on, that the author owns the copyright of code produced with an AI agent under his direction, is a legal question the study cannot settle and Q9 records. The decision that matters is inbound: if the open governor accepts outside contributions under a Developer Certificate of Origin, those contributions can only ever come back into BlarAI under Apache-2.0, which is harmless for an open-core product; if the founder wants to keep the right to relicense everything, he needs a contributor licence agreement with a sublicensing grant, which is the tool of the commercial path and the field's best-known trust cost (HashiCorp's 2023 move to the Business Source License produced the OpenTofu fork; Elastic's 2021 move produced OpenSearch and Elastic added AGPL back in 2024). The Model Context Protocol repository shows the cost of relicensing without an agreement even between two permissive licences: contributions whose authors have not consented stay MIT. Decide the inbound terms before the first outside pull request.

**Where a specification could live.** Two venues were read directly. The OWASP Agent Control Standard's governance file names a project lead and two creators, five workstreams with named leads, weekly triage, and maintainer changes by pull request; contributing costs nothing and needs no OWASP membership; its reference implementation runs Microsoft's Agent Governance Toolkit. The `responsibleai` organisation is a Microsoft-authored contract in a neutral-looking namespace with a single-maintainer governance file and a succession clause, a pattern a solo founder can copy for year one and a warning that "open organisation" is a label, not a structure. Every foundation route trades control for continuity and presumes something a solo project does not yet have. Measured against the entry criteria each one publishes: the new agent-focused foundation under the Linux Foundation has no individual membership tier at all and its lowest corporate tier is ten thousand dollars a year on top of a prerequisite membership, which makes it a poor fit; the Apache Incubator is free and the worst fit, because it wants a champion, mentors and committers from more than one employer; the Eclipse Foundation is the cheapest organisational membership in the comparison at fifteen hundred euros for a micro-company, with individual committer status free; a software-freedom fiscal host prefers mature communities, takes a tenth of processed revenue and takes many months. **The only route whose entry criteria a solo maintainer literally meets today is the Open Source Security Foundation's sandbox stage**, which needs one maintainer, a sponsoring working group and a vote; its next stage needs three maintainers across two organisations, and its highest stage needs five across three plus a third-party audit, so it is enterable alone and not advanceable alone. Two routes cost nothing, need no entity and need no second maintainer: an Internet-Draft at the internet standards body, and a community group at the web standards body.

**The revised recommendation for where the specification lives, and it is cheaper than the one the strand started with.** Year one: no foundation. Contribute the vocabulary into the existing vendor-neutral standard at OWASP, which is free, holds weekly triage, already hosts Microsoft's own reference implementation, and is new enough that there is room to shape it. Back it with a dated Internet-Draft, which costs nothing and exists precisely to establish public, dated prior art, which the copying precedent says is the only protection that has ever worked. Keep the reference implementation permissively licensed in the founder's own organisation. Reserve the security foundation's sandbox for the moment a second independent implementation exists. The trade-off, stated plainly: the vocabulary becomes permissively licensed the moment it is merged, and the standard's name and roadmap stay with its leads, so what this buys is standing and citation, not ownership.

**Community routes (REPORTED).** OpenVINO is a credibility anchor, not a channel: its model server has shipped agentic tool calling since June 2025, with eleven tool parsers and a Model Context Protocol demo, and nothing in the thirty-six repositories of the organisation touches action governance; the best single entry point is a governor-instrumented variant of the model server's agentic demo, contributed upstream where the founder already has standing. ## Q9. Law, liability and regulation in the European Union

*Compiled from the instruments themselves where they could be reached. Nothing was filed, registered or purchased. This is a map of obligations with their triggers, for a lawyer to confirm; it is not legal advice.*

**The expensive instrument is the Cyber Resilience Act, not the Artificial Intelligence Act, and one design decision moves the cost by an order of magnitude.** A governor is a product with digital elements. If it is treated as an ordinary important product it can self-assess; if it is treated as a firewall or an intrusion-prevention system it falls in the higher class where an outside certification body is mandatory with no exception. The implementing regulation defines a firewall as software that monitors and restricts data communication traffic. **Adjudicating an agent's action at the agent's own seam keeps the product out of that class; shipping a packet-level chokepoint that blocks traffic makes "firewall" a live argument.** The vulnerability-reporting duty starts on 11 September 2026, six days before this study, and applies to products already on the market; full application is 11 December 2027. There is also an escape hatch worth knowing: a genuinely free and open-source product in the important class may self-assess under the standard module if its technical documentation is published, which replaces the certification body entirely.

That is the study's strongest convergence, and the two sides are independent. The measurement on this machine says the network seam did not work here, because the installed security suite owns it and the block failed silently. That is one machine, one suite and one interface, and the alternative route of registering as a filtering-platform provider in the governor's own sublayer was not probed, so the bounded claim is that this seam cannot be assumed and must be tested per machine, not that it is universally closed. The regulation says that building the network seam anyway would move the product into the expensive conformity class. A measurement and a statute, arrived at by different strands from different evidence, point at the same design: govern the action at the agent's seam, do not build a packet chokepoint.

**The new Product Liability Directive contains a clause that reads as if written for this product.** In assessing whether a product is defective, account is taken of, in the case of a product whose very purpose is to prevent damage, any failure of the product to fulfil that purpose. Software is a product. Liability is strict and **cannot be excluded by any contract term**, so no licence disclaimer touches it. It covers destruction or corruption of data not used professionally, and it applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026. The split that matters for this product: a wrong allow that moves money is pure economic loss and falls outside the directive, while a wrong allow that destroys a family's photographs falls squarely inside it. That is the failure to insure against and to design against, and it is the reason a wrongful allow, not a wrongful refusal, is the dangerous direction.

**The Artificial Intelligence Act is a demand driver rather than a burden, and the demand just moved.** A deterministic rule engine is not an artificial-intelligence system: the Commission's own guidelines put systems that follow predefined explicit instructions without learning, reasoning or modelling outside the definition as basic data processing. So the product does not carry the Act's obligations unless it adds a model-based layer. The Act's human-oversight provisions are what create demand for governance tooling, and an omnibus that entered into force on 27 July 2026 pushed the high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027, which removes roughly sixteen months of compliance-driven demand from the near term. For a product plan that matters: the regulatory tailwind is real and arrives later than it did before.

**Free does not mean out of scope, and the current licence is the worst of both worlds.** Both the resilience act and the liability directive attach to supply in the course of a commercial activity, which support revenue or a paid tier triggers. And BlarAI's own licence, which forbids commercial use, does not meet either instrument's definition of free and open-source, which requires the freedom to use, modify and redistribute. Publishing a governor under it would give away the source and keep every manufacturer duty. The founder holds the copyright and can choose otherwise, which turns decision point 2 from a preference into a cost question with a regulatory answer.

**Data protection, briefly, because the local-only design does most of the work.** A product that runs entirely on the person's machine and sends nothing puts the vendor outside the controller role for the records it keeps, and the household exemption covers the person's own use. Opt-in diagnostics change that and bring the ordinary duties with them, which is one more reason for the default to be none. Data protection by design is satisfied by construction here rather than by policy.

**The rest of the obligation map, with its triggers.** A legal entity is needed before a paid sale, both to shield liability and because the cheap signing identity is open to European companies and closed to European individuals; formation examples run from a few hundred euros to about two thousand with annual compliance of roughly eight hundred to two and a half thousand. Consumer contract terms cannot disclaim conformity or the updates duty toward consumers, and a digital sale needs the withdrawal-right tick-boxes to be effective. Value-added tax across the Union runs through the one-stop scheme above a ten-thousand-euro threshold. A trade mark is eight hundred and fifty euros for one class and should be registered before the name appears in public. Export control for mass-market cryptography is a self-classification memo rather than a licence. On the gatekeeper question, whether the product could demand access to Windows's agent-protocol authorisation hooks under the interoperability obligation, the textual argument is on point and the operating-system-integrity carve-out, Commission-only enforcement and multi-year timelines make it a negotiating fact rather than a plan. The free move today costs nothing: keep a dated engineering record of every hook Microsoft's own agent surface uses that the public interface does not expose. This study started that record with the filter contract in Q2.

**The three thresholds, as checklists.**

*Before the first free public download:* decide in writing whether this is a commercial activity and then live by that decision; if it is not, use a genuine open-source licence, because the current one buys none of the exclusions; sign the binary anyway, at roughly a hundred and twenty euros a year for an individual certificate; publish the vulnerability-disclosure policy and a support end-date before the first download. Nothing else applies until money or data appears.

*Before open-sourcing:* choose the licence for the regulatory outcome rather than by preference; confirm sole authorship; copy named files rather than directories, because an unrelated third-party checkout sits inside the BlarAI directory; audit the dependency licences; decide which legal person stewards the project, since an individual cannot be a steward under the resilience act; and register the trade mark first.

*Before the first paid sale:* a company; code signing; a consumer contract pack with the withdrawal tick-boxes that actually work for digital content; the value-added-tax one-stop registration before the ten-thousand-euro threshold; a data-protection notice and processing record if diagnostics exist; **a written classification decision under the resilience act, kept as a record**; the resilience-act deliverables built in from the start rather than retrofitted, meaning a software bill of materials, a disclosure policy, secure defaults, a stated support period, the conformity marking and the technical file; **a product-liability quote obtained before the price is set**, because the strict liability cannot be disclaimed; the trade mark; and an export self-classification memo.

## Q10. The governor as a target: its own threat model

**The three threats most likely to bite first, and the first of them is not an attack.**

**It gets skipped rather than defeated.** The measured escapes from seam 1 are the whole story: a command-line flag ran a command the governor was configured to deny without invoking it at all, and a governor that crashes is read as having no opinion so the action proceeds. No agent tells anyone that the governor was absent. That is a product-integrity failure before it is a security one, and the answer is not a stronger lock but liveness detection: the governor must periodically prove it is still in the path, and say so when it is not. This is the same requirement the coverage screen's canary satisfies, which is why that feature is core rather than cosmetic.

**It inherits the antivirus bug class.** Twelve vulnerabilities across seven vendors between 2022 and 2026 were examined, and six of the twelve are the same shape: a privileged service performing a file operation in a directory the user can write, without validating links. One of them is Microsoft's own security agent, and it sits in the United States cyber-defence agency's catalogue of vulnerabilities known to be exploited in the wild. One vendor shipped the same symbolic-link deletion bug three times in three years. A solo founder writing a privileged service that maintains a signed record in a directory the user can reach is walking into that shape deliberately, and the control cannot be code review; it has to be a test that fails when the lock is off.

**The update and signing channel is the highest-value target and the weakest link one person has.** The two canonical supply-chain compromises both show that a signature proves who built something and never what was built. One person with one signing key and one build account is exactly the shape those attacks defeated. Build provenance attested by the build platform and an update framework with offline keys are the answers, and both cost calendar time before the first release rather than after.

**A fourth, which is not an attack and will kill the product just as dead: approval fatigue.** The canonical per-application approval product on the other operating system ships a mode whose stated behaviour is to allow new connections automatically so the person is not interrupted. A governor whose honest answer to "how often will you interrupt me?" is "often" has already lost. Not asking must be the normal case, which is what the lease model and the profile defaults exist for.

**Where the remaining gaps live, and it is not where one would expect.** Most of the unclosable gaps in the threat table are in the agents rather than in the governor: their hook payloads carry no information about the child processes a command will spawn, so a lease cannot be bound to the thing that will actually execute; no decision identifier is echoed back, so replay cannot be detected from the payload alone; no provenance accompanies a request, so the governor cannot show the person why an approval is being asked for; and no agent notifies anything when its own hook configuration changes. Those are the items a specification should ask the agent vendors for, and they are more valuable than anything a third party can build alone.

## Q11. Maintenance, churn and support

**The churn is measured, and it is the number that decides who can maintain this.** Over the twelve months to 2026-09-17, Claude Code published 281 changelog sections, of which 173 touched the hook, permission, sandbox or approval surface, which is 61.6% of those sections and 54.9% of the 315 releases the package registry lists for the same window, and 37 of those carried a bullet that changed the semantics of that surface, which is about three semantic changes a month on one agent. GitHub's command-line agent published 171 releases with 84 touching the same surface, and its shipped build carries eight hook events where the documentation describes fourteen and fails open where the documentation says it fails closed. OpenAI's published 800 releases, 100 of them stable, with 76 touching the surface, over a window that covers only nine and a half months because the release history was rate-limited. Underneath, Windows ships an annual feature update with a twenty-four-month support life for the consumer editions plus an off-cadence release, so three to four concurrent builds must be tested at any time.

The consequence is the single most important operational fact in the study: **the maintenance cost of this product scales with the number of supported agents multiplied by the number of platform branches, and not with the number of users.** One agent adapter against one agent that changes its hook semantics three times a month is a real but bounded commitment for one person. Four adapters is not.

**What that forces [PROPOSED].** A canary call per adapter on a schedule, whose non-interception drops that agent to unknown on the coverage screen, cancels every lease bound to it, and notifies the person regardless of the noise setting. A conformance suite per adapter with a fixture per agent version and a golden payload, run in continuous integration against pinned versions and nightly against the latest, so that a semantic change is caught by a test rather than by a user. Adapters maintained by the community on the open path, with ownership recorded per adapter, because one person cannot track four agents' weekly releases. A support model that is a public issue tracker, a status page and documentation, with no service-level promise. And diagnostics that are opt-in and inspectable as plain text before they are sent, because the local-only promise is the product.

## Q12. Performance, and the budget the measurements force

**The numbers, all measured on this machine.**

| Path | Cost |
|---|---|
| A hook that spawns a process per call | 86 ms median (n=15) |
| A resident governor over a named pipe, including connect | 29 to 34 µs median, 43 to 59 µs at the 95th percentile |
| Caller identity from the pipe | 18.6 µs (one sample) |
| A protocol proxy's own decision | 46 µs median (n=30) |
| A hardware signature, key handle held open | 77 to 94 ms median |
| A hardware signature, opened per call as BlarAI's signer does | 131 to 157 ms median |
| A software signature | 0.027 ms |
| Three kernel event-tracing providers, unfiltered | 290 MB per minute |
| A comparable always-on security suite on this machine | 9 processes, 889.2 MB private bytes (the 958.7 MB working-set sum is recorded in the appendix but working-set sums double-count shared pages and this project measures memory as private or as in-use, never as a sum of working sets) |

**The budget [PROPOSED]: five milliseconds at the 95th percentile per governed action, with a fifty-millisecond degraded ceiling.** Two design consequences follow directly and are not negotiable. The hook must be a thin client that hands the decision to a resident governor, never the governor itself, because a process launch per call costs 86 ms against 34 µs for a pipe, a factor of two and a half thousand. And the record must be chained in software with the hardware signing only the chain heads on a timer, because this chip delivers about ten to twelve signatures a second: a product claiming every decision is hardware-signed would be making a false claim on this hardware, and saying so is the difference between a trust artefact and marketing.

## Q13. Privacy of the record, and shared machines

**The data map.** What is stored: the profile as a file the person owns; the receipts, carrying labels, hashes and the decision rather than the content; the coverage state; and diagnostics only if opted in. Where: per user, not machine-wide. Retention: a default the person can change, with deletion and export both available and uninstall removing the records unless the person asks to keep them. Who can read it: the person, and on a shared machine an administrator can reach other users' files by definition, which is why encryption at rest matters. Protection: the platform's per-user encryption for secrets and the hardware key for the chain's integrity, so tampering is detectable even where reading is possible.

**The multi-user decision [PROPOSED]: per-user profiles and per-user records, one machine-wide enforcement service, and an administrator-set floor that a user may tighten but not loosen.** The alternatives are a single machine-wide profile and record, which is simpler and turns the product into something that reports one family member's activity to another, or a per-user design with no service at all, which is the most private and the weakest, because the governor then runs at the same privilege as the agents it governs and any of them can stop it. The trade-off of the recommendation, stated so it can go on the box: the machine's owner cannot read other accounts' records. That is the price of the product not being spyware, and it should be advertised rather than buried.

**Workplace machines, where this stops being a design question.** Monitoring software on employer-owned machines engages co-determination rules in some member states and the employment provisions of data-protection law generally. The design answer that removes most of that risk is a rule rather than a setting: **the product refuses to run in a configuration where a worker cannot read their own record.** A governor whose records the worker cannot see is a monitoring tool, and this product should decline to be one.

## Q14. Why anyone would trust the governor: the trust artefacts a first release must carry

**The free set, days of work each (REPORTED, sources in appendix B3).** A security policy file plus a `security.txt` (RFC 9116) plus GitHub's private vulnerability reporting, with CVE identifiers issued by GitHub as a numbering authority "usually within 72 hours", so the project need not become one; a software bill of materials in every release (Microsoft's own `sbom-tool` is MIT and produces SPDX 2.2; the Cyber Resilience Act will require one); build provenance through GitHub Artifact Attestations at SLSA Build Level 2 or 3, verifiable with one command; Sigstore signatures over the release files, which Windows does not consume and which therefore complement Authenticode rather than replace it; the OpenSSF Scorecard action and the OpenSSF Best Practices "passing" badge (the silver and gold badges require a bus factor of two and review by someone other than the author, unreachable solo and honest to say so); reproducible pre-signature builds, realistic for .NET and Rust, with the residual (signing and packaging break bit-for-bit identity) stated on the coverage screen; and the two artefacts a solo founder can do better than a funded team because they are honesty rather than money: a shipped claim register in the shape of BlarAI's (claim, status, evidence, stated limitation, the test that blocks and the test that proves it can be switched off) and the coverage screen as the product's contract.

**The cheap set, weeks.** Authenticode signing through Azure Artifact Signing if a company entity in the EU holds the identity (about $9.99 a month), otherwise a certificate from a commercial authority on a hardware token; a Microsoft Store listing (no registration fee); SmartScreen reputation built over weeks of clean installs, with false positives submitted to Microsoft's free file-submission portal.

**Not in year one on the founder's own money.** A paid independent audit: Cure53, Trail of Bits and NCC publish no price list and are GUESSED at several engineer-weeks, tens of thousands of dollars; the free routes (OSTIF, Alpha-Omega, the Sovereign Tech Resilience programme) target widely used or critical projects; GitHub's Secure Open Source Fund pays $10,000 per project on a rolling application and requires community traction and governance. The realistic path is a self-assessment against the CNCF template (which asks for explicit non-goals) in year one and the GitHub fund once there is traction. A kernel-mode component is also not in year one; it needs the Hardware Developer Program and an Extended Validation certificate.

**The cost, GUESSED.** Cash in the low hundreds of dollars for year one (the signing subscription and a domain) if Artifact Signing is available to the entity, a few hundred more for a certificate token if not; founder time on the order of four to six weeks spread across the year, of which the claim register is the largest single item.

A foundation is not a year-one answer; Objective-See's registered charity around one author's tools is the closest structural precedent for a solo security maintainer, and Open Source Collective is the realistic fiscal host once there are donations.

## Q16. The first user

**The protocol [PROPOSED], one page.** Five tasks, think-aloud, with the Lead Architect as the novice first user: install and read the first-run inventory; choose a profile; respond to a first approval prompt; open the coverage screen and say out loud which agent is not governed; open the history and verify it. What is recorded is task completion without help, time, errors, and the words he uses for things, because the vocabulary the product ships should be the vocabulary the first user reaches for. Five users find roughly eighty-five percent of usability problems and three rounds of five beat one round of fifteen, which is why this is a repeated small test rather than one big one.

**One task is must-pass and holds the release.** If he cannot look at the coverage screen and say which agent is not governed, the product's central honesty claim has failed at the only place it is ever delivered, and no amount of correctness underneath compensates. A failure on any other task is a design fix; a failure on that one is a stop.

## Q8. Go or no-go, and the first-year shape

*Out of numerical order deliberately: this question is the conclusion and depends on every other, so it is answered last.*

### What the evidence forces, before any recommendation

Five findings constrain every possible answer, and four of them are measurements rather than readings.

**A governor can see and stop real agent actions on this machine today.** Three seams were driven end to end and worked: the agents' own hook interfaces, a protocol proxy, and a proxy in front of a real local model server. In each case the governor saw the action before it happened and refused it, and the agent behaved as a governed agent should. That is the product's central technical claim, and it is not speculative.

**Every seam a third party can own is cooperative, and one of them failed silently.** The hook can be switched off by a command-line flag, fails open when the governor crashes, and sees a shell command as one opaque string. The protocol proxy is a configuration entry anyone can rewrite. The model-server proxy is an environment variable. And the firewall, the one seam that looks like an operating-system control rather than a courtesy, was accepted by every management surface on this machine and never enforced, because another security product owns it. A governor that does not detect and disclose that will tell people they are protected when they are not.

**The only non-cooperative seam needs a company, a certificate and a kernel driver, and it is the side of the platform the vendor is dismantling.** That route is out of reach for a first year and questionable for any year.

**The platform vendor already ships this product, for enterprises, using the same cooperative seam.** Microsoft Defender's agent runtime protection hooks the same three interfaces this study probed, blocks on them, and is gated behind enterprise licences. Its consumer per-agent surface exists but governs only agents inside Microsoft's own containment model, which the two coding agents on this machine do not use. The gap is real, and it is the gap the hypothesis describes.

**The moat was never going to be technical.** Since every reachable seam is cooperative and the platform vendor can close the gap whenever it chooses, the durable assets are the ones that do not depend on owning a seam: one vocabulary across agents, a record the person owns and can verify, an honest coverage statement, and a specification others implement. Those are exactly what nobody in the surveyed field ships for a person's own machine.

**The regulation and the measurement point the same way, from different directions.** Building the network seam would move the product into the conformity class where an outside certification body is mandatory, because the implementing regulation defines a firewall by exactly what a packet chokepoint does. Independently, the measurement on this machine says that seam silently did not work here, on one machine with one security suite, with the own-provider alternative unprobed. Two strands, two kinds of evidence, one design: govern the action at the agent's seam and never ship the chokepoint. It is also why the liability that matters is a wrongful allow rather than a wrongful refusal, and why the undisclaimable clause in the new product-liability rules, which names products whose very purpose is to prevent damage, is the one legal fact that should shape the product's defaults.

**And the running cost is set by the agents' release cadence, not by users.** Measured over the last twelve months, 61.6% of one agent's 281 releases touched the hook and permission surface, with about three semantic changes a month on that agent alone. Maintenance scales with supported agents multiplied by platform branches. One adapter is a bounded commitment for one person; four is not, which decides both the first year's scope and who maintains adapters after it.

### The recommendation [PROPOSED]

**Go, in the shape the evidence supports rather than the shape the idea started in: publish the specification and an open reference implementation first, and treat the paid product as a decision taken later on evidence, not now on hope.**

The reasoning, in the order that decides it. Trust is the entry ticket and cannot be bought: the recorded objection in the communities that would adopt this is not price but a closed, privileged daemon, and a solo founder shipping security software built with AI agents starts further behind on that than anyone. Open source is how that is answered, and it costs hundreds of euros rather than tens of thousands. The specification is the one asset that survives the platform vendor copying the product, which the precedent says is the likeliest outcome if the work becomes visible at all; being first, dated, public and the reference implementation is the only protection that has ever worked in that situation. The commercial path is not foreclosed by starting open, because the field's own pattern is an open core with a paid tier in a separate directory, and the paid tier can be added when there is a user base to sell to. And the first year's real scarce input is the founder's attention, which is already committed to BlarAI. That is now a measured comparison rather than an intuition: a specification with one reference adapter is estimated at eight to fourteen hours a week, a free product at twenty to thirty falling to twelve to twenty, and both with a paid tier at thirty-five to forty-five, which is full time. The three also differ in a way the hours hide: the specification can be paused for a month, the free product can be paused with an apology, and the paid security product cannot be paused at all, because one that stops shipping updates is a liability still installed on other people's machines.

**What "go" means concretely in year one.** One governed agent to start: Claude Code on this machine through its own hook, because it is measured working, it is the agent that builds BlarAI, and governing the workshop's own tools is a demonstration no vendor can make about itself. One vocabulary, one profile format, one signed local record, one coverage screen that tells the truth about the firewall seam it does not own. A conformance suite so a second implementer is possible. The specification contributed into the existing vendor-neutral standard rather than founded as a rival. No company, no paid tier, no store listing until there is evidence someone wants it.

**What would make this a no-go, stated so it can be checked.** If the platform vendor ships a consumer per-agent action-governance surface covering third-party agents before the reference implementation is public, the product's window closes and the specification is the only thing worth keeping. If the demand test finds paid intent below the stated threshold and the free adoption is negligible, the honest outcome is a specification and a personal tool, not a product. Neither of those is knowable today, which is why the recommendation buys information cheaply rather than committing to a build.

### The minimum lovable thing [PROPOSED]

Not a minimum viable wiring but the smallest artefact that is honest, useful and complete on its own. Seven parts, each of which the study has evidence for.

1. **The vocabulary and its written specification**, with the derivation field that records whether each mapping was declared, parsed, annotated or unknown, because without it the product cannot distinguish a fact from a guess and every honest statement downstream depends on that.
2. **One adapter, for Claude Code, through its own hook**, measured working here including inside subagents, with a conformance fixture pinned to the agent's version.
3. **One profile a person can read aloud**, the developer profile, with the unknown-agent default set to ask.
4. **A signed local record**, chained per action and signed at the head periodically rather than per action, which is what the hardware timing forces: a hardware signature costs 77 to 157 milliseconds and a software one costs 27 microseconds, so per-action hardware signing would cap the machine at about ten actions a second.
5. **The coverage screen**, including the row that says which seams this product does not own, driven by enumerating the registered security products rather than by assumption.
6. **The verification command**, so the person can check their own record rather than trust it.
7. **The claim register**, listing what the product claims, the evidence for each claim, and the limits, in the shape BlarAI already uses.

What is deliberately not in it: a paid tier, a company, a store listing, a second agent adapter, a kernel component, any telemetry, and any network client beyond an opt-in diagnostics path the person can read before it sends.

### The discipline carried over

Fail closed on every error path inside the governor, and say so on screen when the governor itself is degraded rather than degrading quietly. Deny by default for the dangerous classes and ask rather than deny for the ordinary ones, because a governor that denies everything on installation is uninstalled the same afternoon. One engine and many adapters, with no adapter deciding anything. Labels and hashes in the record by default, and arguments only where a rule needs them. Every control shipped with a test that proves it blocks and a test that proves it fails when the control is switched off, so that "secure" and "the test cannot reach it" are never confused. And the product's own posture changes are explicit acts by the person, recorded, with the disabling of the governor itself the loudest record of all.

### What "adopted" would be measured by

Four numbers, and the second is the one that matters. Installs and weekly active machines, which measure reach. **The share of agent actions on a governed machine that are actually governed rather than merely observed**, which is the product's real claim and the one number a competitor cannot fake. Agents governed per machine, which measures whether the adapter set is keeping up with the agents people run. And, on the specification side, the count of implementations that are not the founder's, because that is the threshold every foundation, every standards route and the likeliest good outcome with the platform vendor all require.

## What was read, and what was not verified

**Method.** Ten strands ran in parallel: five probing seams on this machine and five researching. Two usage limits interrupted the work, the first killing nine strands mid-run and the second killing four more; every strand was either resumed from the artefacts it had left on disk or re-run, and two seams were compiled by this session directly from the raw probe data because their agents did not survive to write a report. The compiler re-derived every conclusion rather than inheriting a summary, and re-ran one predecessor's registry searches after finding that its commands had been malformed and had therefore established nothing. Machine state was verified clean at the start of the work, after the first interruption and after the last probe.

**One question two strands answered differently, and how it was settled.** The design strand could not tell whether BlarAI's live path hashes the actual argument values or only their schema, because its docstring and one research document disagree. The code-reading strand had read the caller and found that the orchestrator passes the real tool arguments into the canonical action. The code wins, and the consequence is carried into the product's design: a governor of third-party agents will see real arguments in every action and in every record derived from one, so each adapter must decide what is stored and what is only hashed. This is recorded because two strands agreeing would have been weaker evidence than one of them reading the caller.

**Not verified, in the order it would matter to a decision.**

- **Whether a non-Microsoft package registering the agent-protocol filter contract would be loaded.** Testing it needs a built and installed package, which the rules forbade, and the contract is undocumented, so it may be gated on signature or publisher. The finding is what the artefact shows, not a route.
- **Whether a governor's own network-filter provider would be honoured** beside the installed security suite's top-weight layer. The firewall failure is measured; the workaround is not.
- **The other two agents' hooks were never run.** One account's policy refused a harmless probe and the other agent is not installed, so everything about them is read from their shipped code and their documentation, where those two disagree about failure behaviour.
- **Several isolation levers were deliberately not exercised**: the Windows sandbox needs a feature enable and a restart, an application-control policy would be a security-settings change, and one container child-process test failed on a working-directory error before it measured anything.
- **The event-tracing cost figure is confounded.** Other agents and the security suite ran throughout and were not held fixed; the volume figure is trustworthy and the processor figure is a direction only.
- **The consumer-security market has no single instrument.** Two live surveys disagree by construction, 74.5% paying against 61% on free products, because one sampled visitors to a testing site and the other sampled the general population; the most-cited share report excludes Microsoft by design and its data is from 2023.
- **Whether any insurance policy pays out when this kind of product fails open** was not established, and it is a question for a broker before release.
- **The independent-review price range is derived from aggregates, not quoted**, and no firm published a rate.
- **One agent's release history is incomplete** at nine and a half months of the twelve, because the source rate-limited the rest; one performance figure is a single sample; Windows on Arm was not probed at all; the boot cost of a service was not measured.
- **The copyrightability of code written with an artificial-intelligence agent** is the premise under the whole relicensing question, and no jurisdiction's answer was established. It is a question for a lawyer, not a fact this study produced.
- **The member state is not chosen**, so every formation figure is an example rather than a plan, and the multi-user consent posture remains yours.
