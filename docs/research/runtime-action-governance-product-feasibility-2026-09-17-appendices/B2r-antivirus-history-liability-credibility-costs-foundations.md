## 0. Outcome first — the prediction

**What the antivirus history predicts for a third-party governor if Microsoft ships a native consumer equivalent.** It predicts that the category does not die, that the *paid consumer* version of it does, and that the survivors are the ones whose promise Microsoft structurally cannot make. Defender did not destroy the antivirus industry: Gen Digital still has 79 million paying customers and $5.0 billion of revenue (FY26). What it destroyed was the reason a normal person pays a stranger for Windows protection — by 2026, 53% of United States computer users rely on the operating system's built-in security, free antivirus use is back to 61% of antivirus users, and Microsoft Defender is the single most-used product at 23% of them, down from 28% two years earlier only because the free pool grew around it. The money that remained went three ways, all of them away from the original product: into identity and finance bundles (Gen bought LifeLock in 2017 and MoneyLion in 2025; McAfee sells identity protection, deepfake detection and scam detection), into cross-platform household subscriptions that Windows-only Defender cannot serve, and into geography and jurisdiction (Kaspersky, banned in the United States from 2024, is still first in Asia and South/Central America and absent from North America's top twelve). On the **commercial path**, a third-party consumer governor should therefore expect the same shape: if Microsoft ships a native consumer per-agent control, the standalone paid Windows-only product has no defensible price, because Defender's precedent is that "good enough and already there" wins the 90% and leaves a paid remainder that is either cross-platform, business-managed, or explicitly positioned against the platform vendor itself. The one niche Defender never took and never could is the privacy-positioned one — and it is the strongest fit here, because the agents a governor must watch include Microsoft's own, and Microsoft cannot credibly sell the product that constrains Copilot. On the **open-source path**, the prediction is different and better: Microsoft shipping the feature does not kill an open specification, it validates it. The AppGet record (R3 §4a) says a permissive licence never protected a design from re-implementation — but it also says the thing Microsoft re-implemented was a *design*, and the person who published the design first, dated, with a conformance suite and a second implementer, is the reference rather than the casualty. The open path's realistic ceiling is standing, not revenue; the commercial path's realistic ceiling is a donation-and-supporter-licence business of the Sandboxie-Plus or simplewall size (€40–€80 supporter certificates, Patreon, no charge for the download), because every Windows per-app governor that exists today is free. The honest joint prediction is: **build the specification and the open core for standing; do not build a first-year business plan that requires consumers to pay for a Windows-only governor, because the antivirus history says that is exactly the thing Defender's free baseline removed from the market.**

Four further outcomes, stated plainly:
1. **The market-share instrument is broken for this question.** The most-cited Windows anti-malware market-share report — OPSWAT's — deliberately *excludes* Microsoft, and its current edition was generated in May 2023. The two live consumer surveys disagree by construction: AV-Comparatives' 2026 survey (self-selected visitors to an antivirus testing site, 68.7% advanced or expert users) reports 74.5% paying for desktop security; Security.org's 2026 report (a US general-population panel) reports 61% of antivirus users on free products. Any statement about "the consumer security market" inherits whichever instrument produced it. Both are in §1.1.
2. **The measured support load of comparable indie Windows security tools is 1.6 to 4.9 new GitHub issues per week** at 9,000–21,000 stars (§2.1). That is survivable solo; what is not measured anywhere, and what a paid product adds, is the non-technical e-mail inbox.
3. **The credential asymmetry is stark.** For the segment most likely to install first — developers — one merged upstream pull request is worth more than the AIGP certification, and both are worth less than a reproducible build and an independent reviewer (§3). The recorded objection in this market is not to unqualified founders but to closed source and AI-written privileged code.
4. **Year-one cash is small and year-one attention is not.** €1,000–€1,500 lean, €3,500–€5,000 mid, €12,000–€25,000 with a trademark and a paid review (§4.1). The attention estimate is 8–14 hours a week for the specification alone, 12–30 for a free product, and 35–45 — full time — for both with a paid tier (§4.2).

### 1.5 The prediction, restated at length

If Microsoft ships a native consumer per-agent action-governance surface — R2-C puts a full cross-vendor consumer version at 9–24 months, median ~15, with the engine already shipping inside the Defender Antivirus platform and gated only by licence — then:

**On the commercial path.** Expect the Defender pattern compressed into two years rather than ten, because the seam is younger and Microsoft already owns it in the enterprise. Specifically: (i) the free consumer default takes the "does not think about this" majority immediately, as it did for antivirus, and that majority is the only one big enough to support a consumer price; (ii) the paid remainder goes where Defender structurally cannot follow — cross-platform, privacy-positioned, and managed-for-a-business — and a Windows-only single-founder product can credibly occupy only the second of those; (iii) pricing collapses toward the observed Windows floor, which is €0 with a €40–€80 supporter tier, not the €40–€60 first-year antivirus price, because the comparables that set expectations in this category (simplewall, TinyWall, Portmaster free tier, Windows Firewall Control) are free and the one paid one-time governor, Little Snitch at $59, is on the other operating system; (iv) the first-year-discount-then-renewal-shock model that funds Norton is unavailable to a one-person shop with no brand and no reputation to spend; and (v) the durable asset is not the binary but the position — being the named reference for how agent actions are governed, which is what Sysinternals had and what AppGet did not.

**On the open-source path.** Microsoft shipping the feature is a *validating* event, not a terminal one, for three reasons the antivirus history also shows. First, the platform's own control surface creates the vocabulary problem that a specification solves: Microsoft's enterprise product already consumes four different vendors' bespoke hook interfaces (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Copilot CLI, the Copilot app — R2-C §1a), and nothing makes those one interface. Second, the open competitor to a bundled default survives on the thing the bundle cannot carry — openness itself; the recorded objection in this market is to closed privileged daemons (R2-G, the Unfucked thread, 2026-02-26), which is an advantage a platform vendor cannot copy. Third, the continuity precedents (Sandboxie → Sandboxie-Plus; Classic Shell → Open-Shell; TrueCrypt → VeraCrypt) say an open project outlives both its author and its competitor's product decisions, while a closed one does not. The realistic outcome on this path is standing and a small donation base, not revenue: the measured comparables are 8,982 stars and donations only (simplewall), 19,451 stars and Patreon plus €40–€80 certificates (Sandboxie-Plus), 37,668 stars and site advertising (Rufus). None of them is a living.

**The line that decides which path.** If the founder's goal is income, the antivirus record says a Windows-only consumer governor is the wrong product and the EU small-business console is the only paid niche a solo vendor could enter — into direct competition with a Microsoft bundle. If the goal is standing, portfolio and a governance credential, the open specification path is the one the same record supports, and Microsoft shipping the feature makes it *more* valuable, not less, provided the specification is dated, public and implemented by somebody other than its author first.

### 2.2 The liability shape, in one paragraph

The dangerous failure for a governor is not the false alarm, it is the **wrongful allow**: the product is sold as a control, the user relies on it and stops watching, and the loss — a payment made, a repository pushed, a mailbox exported — is caused by someone else's agent through a gate that was advertised as closed. Three exposures stack on that single event, and the law strand (R4law) owns the detail: **contractual and consumer**, where the Digital Content Directive's conformity and update duties apply to paid software supplied to consumers and where limitation-of-liability clauses against a consumer are narrowly constrained; **product liability**, where the recast Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 brings software expressly within the definition of a product and treats destruction or corruption of data as recoverable damage, with the development-risk defence unavailable in the way a software maker would want when it keeps shipping updates; and **regulatory**, where the Cyber Resilience Act (EU) 2024/2847 turns anyone engaged in commercial activity into a "manufacturer" carrying vulnerability-handling, CE-marking and — since 2026-09-11, six days before this study — actively-exploited-vulnerability reporting duties, while an unpaid open-source steward carries a lighter set (R3 §5.1). The commercial reading, which is the only thing this strand adds: **the moment a euro changes hands the product crosses from steward to manufacturer**, and that crossing is the largest single cost in the business case — paid in obligations rather than invoices, and unavoidable by any structure that also collects money. Two consequences follow directly and are free to act on: the **claim register and coverage screen** (R3 §5.1) are simultaneously the trust artefact and the liability defence, because a documented non-goal is not a broken promise; and every word of marketing copy that says "blocks" where the honest word is "asks" converts a technical limitation into a misrepresentation.

## 3. Founder credibility

### 3.1 How the two credentials read to each segment

**The facts first.** AIGP: exam **$799 non-member / $649 IAPP member**; IAPP annual professional membership **$295**; for non-members the initial certification maintenance fee is included in the exam price and **$250** is due at recertification (REPORTED, https://store.iapp.org/aigp-exam/, read 2026-09-17). The IAPP's own certification page describes it as demonstrating competence in AI development, ethical deployment and AI management best practice, covering foundational AI knowledge, legal frameworks, lifecycle and risk governance, and emerging issues; it does not state a launch date, holder count or prerequisites (REPORTED, https://iapp.org/certify/aigp/). Roughly **4,000 holders worldwide** as of 2026 and a 2024 launch — REPORTED (secondary), search summary of training-vendor pages, low confidence. OpenVINO upstream contributions: a public, verifiable commit history in Intel's inference runtime (the founder's GitHub identity and upstream-contributor status are on record in the project's own CLAUDE.md; no external verification was performed in this strand).

| Segment | AIGP reads as… | OpenVINO upstream contributions read as… |

### 3.2 What a solo, non-technical founder shipping AI-built software must show

Ordered by the objection each one answers. The objections are not hypothetical: they are the ones recorded in parts/R2-G-demand.md §8.1 against tools of exactly this shape.

1. **Open source the privileged component.** This is the entry ticket, not a differentiator. The Unfucked thread (2026-02-26) shows a local-first tool with a good idea rejected *before evaluation* because the daemon was closed and had full filesystem access. A governor has strictly more privilege than that. Cost: €0. Licence: Apache-2.0 on the core per R3 §0.
2. **Reproducible builds plus published attestation.** Answers "did this binary come from that source?", which is the question open source alone does not answer. Realistic on .NET and Rust to a pre-signature hash; signing and MSIX packaging break bit-for-bit identity, so publish the pre-signature hash and say so on the coverage screen (R3 §5.1). Cost: €0, days of work. GitHub Artifact Attestations give SLSA Build Level 2 free; `cosign sign-blob` free.
3. **An independent review by a named human who did not write the code.** Answers "who checked the AI's work?", which is the specific objection to AI-built security software. The paid route is not affordable in year one (§4.1); the **free routes** recorded in R3 §5.1 are: the **GitHub Secure Open Source Fund** ($10,000 per project — $6,000 during a three-week, 15-hour programme plus $2,000 at six and twelve months — plus $10,000 Azure credits; rolling applications; teams of up to two maintainers; requires "community traction and adoption" and governance), **OSTIF** (donor-funded, no open application, targets widely used projects), **Sovereign Tech Resilience** (critical open digital infrastructure, rolling, "waiting time will vary"), and **Alpha-Omega** (critical projects and foundations). All four require traction the project does not have on day one, which makes the ordering: ship, gather users, then apply.
4. **A claim register and an in-product coverage screen.** Answers "what does it *not* stop?" — the question every honest reviewer asks and every marketing page dodges. R3 §5.1 identifies this as the one artefact a solo founder can execute better than a funded team, because it costs honesty rather than money, and §2.2 above shows it doubles as the liability defence.
5. **A published disclosure process.** `SECURITY.md`, `/.well-known/security.txt` (RFC 9116, Contact and Expires required), GitHub private vulnerability reporting enabled; GitHub is a CVE Numbering Authority and issues CVE identifiers for public repositories on request, so no CNA application is needed. Cost: €0, hours.
6. **Disclose the AI authorship rather than let it be discovered. [PROPOSED]** The yolo-cage thread shows commenters detecting AI-written code and documentation and treating the detection itself as the disqualifier. A short, specific statement — which parts were AI-assisted, what review each got, what the test coverage is, what a human verified by hand — converts a discovered flaw into a stated method. Untested: no precedent was found of a security project doing this successfully, so this is a judgement, not a pattern.
7. **A written governance and succession file from day one** (`GOVERNANCE.md` with a named second owner, a GitHub account successor, a `CONTINUITY.md` naming the install identifiers). R3 §6.3 has the six-item kit; the relevance to *credibility* rather than continuity is that a privileged security tool with a bus factor of one and no plan is a risk a careful user can see, and a plan they can read is the cheapest answer to it.

### 4.2 Attention — hours per week in the first year

All GUESSED, with the reasoning given, and stated in the form the founder asked for. The load-bearing inputs are the measured issue rates in §2.1, R2-D's signing and reputation timelines, and R3 §2.1's description of the OWASP ACS weekly triage cadence.

**If you choose the specification only** — publish the contract, the payload schema, the conformance vectors; contribute into the OWASP Agent Control Standard's workstreams; no shipped Windows product, no company, no money changing hands — **expect 8 to 14 hours a week**, sustained, for the year.
- Writing and revising the specification and its conformance vectors: 4–6 h.
- Workstream participation: 2–3 h (ACS triages weekly on a call, with `scope:`/`priority:`/`workstream:` labels — the cadence is somebody else's, and missing it costs standing).
- Keeping a small reference implementation honest against the vectors: 2–3 h.
- Correspondence, issue replies on the spec repository, release notes: 1–2 h.
- *Why this is the low number:* there is no support inbox, no release cadence tied to four vendors' hook changes, no legal entity, no insurance, no CRA exposure, and no user who can lose money. The load is bounded by other people's meeting calendar, not by adoption.
- *What it buys:* dated public prior art, and a seat in the venue where Microsoft's own reference implementation already sits. *What it does not buy:* revenue, users, or protection from re-implementation (AppGet, R3 §4a).

**If you choose the product only** — ship a free, open-source Windows governor; no specification work; no paid tier — **expect 20 to 30 hours a week for the first six months, settling to 12 to 20 hours a week thereafter.**
- The first six months are front-loaded by things that have nothing to do with the code: MSIX or installer packaging, the signing identity (identity validation 1–20 business days), SmartScreen reputation build-up ("several weeks and hundreds of clean installs", R2-D §3), and a false-positive submission round to Microsoft, Kaspersky, Bitdefender, Avast/Norton and ESET before launch — a governor brushes against Microsoft's "Tampering software" and "Evasion software" classification criteria by design.
- Of the settled 12–20 h: **issue triage 3–6 h** (the measured 1.6–4.9 issues per week at 20–45 minutes each, including reproducing on a Windows build you may not have); **release engineering 3–5 h**; **signing and false-positive maintenance 1–2 h**; **documentation and the coverage screen 2–4 h**; everything else 2–3 h.
- *The number to watch is not users.* The load is roughly linear in **supported agents × Windows servicing branches**, because the hook contracts underneath move: Claude Code, Codex CLI, Copilot CLI and VS Code all changed their pre-action hook surfaces during 2026 (R3 §3). Each additional supported agent is a permanent recurring cost, not a one-off integration.

**If you choose both, with a paid tier** — specification plus product plus a commercial SKU — **expect 35 to 45 hours a week for the whole first year.** That is full time, and it does not fit around anything else.
- Everything in the product path at its settled rate (12–20 h), plus the specification limb at a reduced 4–6 h.
- **Company administration, bookkeeping and filings: 1–2 h.**
- **CRA posture, vulnerability handling and the disclosure process: 1–2 h**, rising sharply and unpredictably the week anything is reported.
- **Paid-customer support: 3–6 h** — the GitHub figures do not cover this. A person who paid expects an answer in days; a GitHub issue does not. GUESSED at 3–6× the GitHub volume (§2.1).
- **Billing, refunds, licence recovery: ~1 h.**
- **Trust artefacts and claim-register upkeep: 1–2 h** — every shipped change can invalidate a row in the register, and a stale register is worse than none.
- *The asymmetry to say out loud:* path (A) can be paused for a month and nobody is harmed; path (B) can be paused with an apology; **path (C) cannot be paused at all**, because a security product that stops shipping updates is a liability that is still installed on other people's machines — and under the CRA, from 2026-09-11, an unpatched actively exploited vulnerability in a commercially supplied product is a reporting obligation, not an embarrassment.

### 5.1 Recommendation, and its trade-off

**Recommendation.** For year one, do not join a foundation; **contribute into the OWASP GenAI Security Project's Agent Control Standard, and back it with a dated IETF Internet-Draft.** The ACS is free, requires no legal entity and no second maintainer, triages weekly, already hosts Microsoft's own reference implementation, and is small and young enough (127 stars, created 2026-04-10) that a Windows-seam profile, a receipt format and a coverage-disclosure schema land as workstream items rather than as a rival standard — while the Internet-Draft costs nothing, publishes within days, never expires from the archive, and converts the design into public dated prior art in an archive no company controls. Keep the reference implementation in the founder's own GitHub organisation under Apache-2.0 with `GOVERNANCE.md`, `SECURITY.md` and a named succession clause (R3 §0), and reserve the foundation move — **OpenSSF Sandbox is the one whose entry criteria a solo maintainer actually meets today** — for the moment a second independent implementation exists, because that is the asset every foundation in this table is actually screening for. Treat the Eclipse Foundation as the fallback if a European legal home and Cyber Resilience Act stewardship become the deciding factors, since its €1,500 micro-company Contributing tier is the only affordable organisational membership found anywhere in this comparison.

**The trade-off, stated plainly.** Contributing into ACS means the vocabulary becomes Apache-2.0 and CC BY-SA 4.0 the moment it merges, and the standard's name, roadmap and promotion authority stay with its existing leads — the founder gets standing and citation, not ownership, and cannot later withdraw the contribution or fork the brand. The alternative, founding a standard in his own organisation, keeps the name and the roadmap and buys nothing else: a specification with one implementer and one author is not a standard, it is a README, and R3 §4 records that every specification Microsoft has adopted had more than one implementer and a permissive reference implementation first. The price of the recommended path is exclusivity on the vocabulary; what it buys is the only thing that has ever protected a solo author at this kind of seam — being first, dated, public, and the reference.
