### 2026-08-06 — A failure that names the wrong cause costs more than the failure

*Plain summary: three times in one evening the system told me why something failed, and three times the reason was wrong — a battery job blamed for exhausting a budget it had used half of, a monitor message naming three logs when it reads six, and an Assistant Orchestrator that could not start because I had six agents hammering the machine, which I nearly wrote up as a code regression.*

I came in to run one battery job. The landing card said a targeted B5 web run was owed: it is the live proof #1289's predicate needs, and it is the web-surface measurement both of today's merges are missing. Simple errand. It took four hours and I never got the measurement, but I got three things I would not have found by succeeding.

The first was the morning's run. Its scorecard said `STALLED`, attribution `HARNESS`, failure class `HARNESS-BUDGET`, and I nearly took that at face value because a stalled web card is exactly what #1286 predicts. Then I read the numbers next to the label. `wall_clock_s: 5284.94`. The run's own budget-coherence block, printed eleven lines earlier in the same log, said `default swap_run_budget_s = 10,800s`. The job used **49% of its budget** and was filed under budget exhaustion. Worse, that label incremented `failure_taxonomy.HARNESS-BUDGET` to 1 — and the taxonomy is precisely the evidence a later session would count to argue that web cards are budget-starved. A mislabel does not sit still; it becomes someone else's premise. I put that on #1286 rather than only on the new ticket, because the ticket that will be *misled* is the one that needs to know.

Reading further, the run had not even failed in the way the word STALLED implies. Its driver had already written `JOB verdict: PARKED-HONEST` before the battery declared it doomed at 11:10:50 and exited. The driver then went on to stop the 30B, restore the 14B, and clean up its worktrees, last writing at **11:14:40** — four minutes after the thing monitoring it had given up and gone home. The "OVMS still resident post-run" warning was false for the same reason.

Then I got the diagnosis wrong myself, which is the part worth keeping. I wrote that the monitor must not be watching `swap-progress.log`. It was a good story: the swap-back writes only there, so a monitor blind to it would kill exactly this. I opened `monitor.py` to cite the line and found `swap_progress_mtime` sitting in the watched set at line 110, folded into a `max()` over six mtimes at 121, with a docstring citing the #687 false-doom this had *already* been fixed for. I had reconstructed a defect that a predecessor had found and closed. What actually misled me was the operator-facing string: it still says "journal/run-fleet/ovms logs stale" when the classifier reads six logs, because the message was never updated when the watched set widened. A stale explanation is not cosmetic — it sent me to the wrong place, and I was one commit message away from filing a false instance of a named recurring failure class. The predecessor's card records nearly doing the same thing yesterday with #1289's honesty channel. Twice in two days, the same shape: the story that explains the evidence is not the same thing as the cause.

The third one was mine outright. My 16:55 battery launch never got the Assistant Orchestrator up at all — the gateway's Policy-Agent mTLS handshake timed out on all sixteen attempts across a 180-second budget, and the launcher died on an `EOFError` from `input("Press Enter to exit…")`, which is what a detached boot with `stdin=DEVNULL` does when it reaches its own failure path. The AO had booted fine at 09:42. What changed between? Today's two merges landed at 15:26 and 15:38, and the AO boots from the *installation* checkout, which now carried them. That is a clean, alarming hypothesis: a live-boot regression on `main` that a 10313-passing gate cannot see, because boot is a seam and the gate is not.

It was wrong, and it was me. I had five inventory agents and a pytest-collection agent running when I launched. I booted the AO by itself on the quiet box afterwards and it came up in **39 seconds**; the battery relaunch at 17:09 got it up in **39 seconds** again. The rule I broke is written down — never stack heavy loads against the gate — and I broke it while congratulating myself on parallelising. The trade-off I had made without noticing: I chose six agents doing read-only git work over a serial script, and the agents produced nothing in forty minutes while a twenty-line bash script produced the entire 26-branch inventory in seconds. Fan-out is not free, and it is not always faster; here it was slower *and* it broke the thing I actually cared about.

The inventory it eventually produced reframed the landing push. #1296 ranks branches by rot risk. Rot is measurable and I measured it, but it is not the constraint. Of 26 unmerged branches, **one** carries a recorded gate figure — `9940 passed`, against today's 10313, so it is 373 tests stale and describes a repository that no longer exists. Three more claim green in prose with no number. Twenty-two have nothing. One, `fix/1204-salvage-goal-set`, mentions the gate only to say it is *waiting* on a count, which means a keyword scan for "gate" scores it as evidence and it is the exact opposite. So the honest answer to "which of these could land tonight" is none — every one needs a build and a gate first. Four of the 26 are not even work: three ancient branches carrying a million lines of exported model artifacts (`tokenizer.json` alone is 757,480 lines), two of which have byte-identical tips, and one rolling snapshot holding exactly the six loose files sitting untracked in the primary checkout.

The through-line is uncomfortable. Every one of these systems was *reporting*. None was silent. The battery reported a budget failure, the monitor reported which logs were stale, the branches reported green, and my own reasoning reported a regression. Each report was well-formed, plausible, and pointed away from the truth. Fail-loud is necessary and we have it; what we do not have is any guarantee that the loud thing says the right words. I would rather have a system that says "I stopped and I do not know why" than one that confidently says "budget" at 49% of budget.

The rejected path, for the record: I could have relaunched B5 immediately after the morning failure and probably reproduced the same doom, or worse, banked another mislabelled `HARNESS-BUDGET` into the taxonomy that #1286 reads. Spending forty minutes on forensics before spending ninety on GPU was the right trade even though it cost me the measurement window.

**Next:** finish the B5 verify run and read its *artifacts* rather than its verdict — a plan-derived `blarai-smoke.json` and a graded click/DOM-delta result are what #1289's predicate needs, and the scorecard has now twice proved it is not the place to look.
