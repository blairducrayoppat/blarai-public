### 2026-08-07 — A floor lowered past its own evidence, and the lock that would have lied about it

*Plain summary: the Lead Architect told me to drop the coder's memory safety floor from 20 GiB to 19 GiB because it was blocking work. The number was easy. The hard part was recording honestly what kind of number 19 is — and I got that wrong on the first pass, in exactly the dimension the change was about.*

The instruction was one sentence: lower the threshold for the coder to 19 GiB, 20 is too high and blocking efforts. What made it interesting was not the arithmetic.

The floor is the last check between a coder dispatch and a machine with too little memory to hold the 30B model. Before the swap driver tears down the 14B assistant and loads the coder, it reads available RAM and refuses if there is not enough. It is not one number, either — `default.toml`'s comment on the key says so outright: keep it in lockstep with `start-llm`'s `$needGB` and the battery's `$LEAN_GATE_GIB`, which is defined as the gate plus 0.5. Grepping turned up a fourth site the comment did not mention: `SwapDriver.__init__` carries its own `gate_gb` default whose inline comment *claims* it matches `start-llm`. So changing three numbers would have left a fourth quietly asserting a lockstep that no longer held. That one came in scope not because it was broken, but because my change was what would break it.

Except I found the wrong fourth site. The reviewer traced every construction of the driver and showed that the default I had so carefully updated is **never reached by a gate decision** — the production path rehydrates from a spec and always passes `gate_gb` explicitly. The fallback that *can* actually fire is `swap_ops.py`'s `spec.get("gate_gb", 20.0)`, and I had left it at 20.0. So the lockstep sweep I wrote up as complete had corrected a dead constant and missed the live one — and the test I wrote to guard fallbacks inspected the dead one too. Finding *a* site that matches the pattern is not the same as finding the site that decides.

The part I want to remember is the provenance. This floor moved once before, in #777 on 2026-07-09, from 21 to 20 — and that move was **measured**: a supervised 30B load from a 19.85 GiB ambient reached READY in about thirteen seconds with no page storm. The 21 before it had been an inference wearing a measurement's clothes, inherited from a 2026-06-21 dataset whose lowest observed success was 23.1 GiB and which had never tested below that. #777 is a good piece of work; it turned a guess into data.

Nineteen is not that. There is no measurement at 19; there is an operator who is tired of dispatches being refused and who accepted the risk.

And here I got it wrong, in the one dimension this change was supposedly about. I wrote — into the config comment, the ADR amendment, the decision register, this fragment, and a commit message — that 19 sits below *the lowest ambient anyone has ever measured a successful 30B load from*. The independent reviewer went to `docs/performance/` and found that our own dataset says otherwise: #784's probe reached READY from a **9.83 GiB** ambient with the 14B still resident, in 34.8 seconds against the clean path's 15.5. That run is non-representative — a wrong certs root meant the AO was never stopped — but the superlative I wrote was simply false, and I had written it five times while congratulating myself on epistemic hygiene. The true claim is narrower and duller: 19 is below the lowest ambient at which a *supervised, post-step-aside* load has been recorded, which is the measurement point this gate actually uses. Worse, the evidence I omitted was evidence **for** the operator's directive — under headroom pressure this substrate stages slower rather than hard-failing, which is the real reason a thin floor is survivable. I had the strongest argument for the change sitting in the repo and replaced it with a false absolute. A claim that flatters your framing is the one to go and check. 

What survives the correction is the original point. Both moves change the same constant, and it would have been effortless to write them up in the same voice. So the config comment, the ADR amendment, and the register row all say plainly that the current value is a directive and not a measurement, and name the exact distance between the setting and the nearest comparable evidence. The trade-off I recorded is an asymmetry rather than a reassurance: the gate aborts gracefully and restores the assistant, and the load is bounded by a 480-second deadline, so a floor set too thin costs a failed dispatch — while a floor set too thick costs every dispatch the operator cannot run, which is the live, recurring, observed cost that prompted the change. That is why the lower floor is defensible. Not because 19 is safe; because being wrong at 19 is cheap and being wrong at 20 was not.

The second lesson came from the other session working this tree, and it was the better catch of the day. I had planned a regression lock. The obvious one asserts `swap_min_free_gb == 19.0`. That assertion passes whether or not a single line of code ever reads the value — it is the built-but-wired-into-nothing shape, and they told me it had produced three separate defects here in two days, including a red gate that morning. So the lock drives a real `SwapDriver` through its real step-8 gate and asserts on outcomes: refuses at floor minus 0.1, admits at floor plus 0.1, with the threshold read from the shipped config rather than restated in the test. Then, per the principle that every control ships with proof it blocks when engaged, it moves the gate above a previously-admitted reading and requires the same reading to flip to refused — which is what separates "the driver enforces the floor" from "the driver admits everything."

I did not trust that on inspection. I mutated `swap_driver.py` to disable the gate comparison outright and re-ran: two of the six tests failed, including the tested-off one. Then I restored and re-ran the full driver suite, 74 passing. A lock you have not seen fail is a lock you are guessing about, and the `== 19.0` version would have sailed straight through that same broken gate.

Two smaller things worth banking. I reported to the operator that available RAM was 9.85 GiB and that the floor might therefore not be his real problem — I had sampled it at exactly the moment the other session's battery probe was loading the 30B, and reported the dip as the resting state. It was 22.66 GiB. Measuring a headroom change from a single sample taken during someone else's memory-heavy operation is the same error as narrating elapsed time instead of reading the clock. And when `verify-battery-ao-lifecycle` came back 89 passed / 1 failed, I ran it against unmodified main before assuming anything: identical failure, pre-existing, a stale assertion expecting two `Write-SkipReport` call sites and counting seven. It is now #1314 rather than a line in a chat message, along with #1315 for the stale 21.0 fallbacks I deliberately did not sweep into this change — a fallback higher than the configured floor is merely conservative, and the direction that would actually be a defect is now closed by the new lock.

**Proposed lesson:** A superlative ("the lowest ever", "nothing has ever") is a CLAIM about a dataset, and it must be checked against that dataset before it ships — most urgently when it flatters the framing of your own change, because that is when it will not be reread.

**Proposed lesson:** A threshold that moves for a *reason* and a threshold that moves on *evidence* must not be written up in the same voice — record the distance between the setting and the nearest measurement, or the next reader inherits a judgement call as data.

**Proposed lesson:** A regression lock on a configured safety value must assert on BEHAVIOUR at both sides of the boundary, never on the constant — and must be seen to fail against a deliberately broken gate before it is trusted, because the constant-equality version passes against exactly the defect it was written to catch.
