### 2026-07-23 — The fifth attempt worked because it stopped asking "is there a negation?" and started asking "is this whole thing a negation?"

*Plain summary: four designs tried to spot a negation word next to a success phrase and let the sentence through; English has infinitely many ways to flip meaning, so all four leaked. The fix was to invert who has to prove what — and while measuring it I found the shipped guard had been letting "The run successfully completed" through on failed runs the whole time.*

The coordinator's prose guard exists to stop the 14B writing "the run completed successfully" about a run that failed. It works by looking for success phrases and refusing them. The cost of that bluntness is that it also throws away true sentences — "the overall run did not complete successfully" is accurate and was being dropped, once in every thirty-four cycles.

Four designs went at that in one day and all four were rejected by independent review. I inherited the wreckage with a handoff brief that opened by telling me the session that wrote it was contaminated on this ticket, had published a "73% improvement" that turned out to be an artefact of grading its own homework, and had written a safety proof onto the ticket that was simply invalid. The brief's instruction was: do not use my conclusions as premises. That is the most useful thing a predecessor has ever handed me, and I want to record that the *retraction* was worth more than the four attempts.

The four designs share one shape, and once you see it you cannot unsee it: each searched for a negation marker near the claim, and granted the excuse when it found one. That makes the accept side open. Every construction the marker list does not anticipate falls through to *accepted* — the expensive direction. The reviewers did not need cleverness to break them, only English: *"the claim that the run did not complete successfully is false"*, *"it is a myth that…"*, *"the build did not complete successfully until the retry."* All three mean the run succeeded. None is reachable by adding another word to a list, because polarity gets flipped by open-class vocabulary and by presupposition, and neither is enumerable. The fourth design's own author's fix was defeated by moving one adjective to the end of the sentence, found in under ten minutes.

So v5 inverts the burden. A claim is excused only if the segment holding it is consumed *in full* by one of eight closed grammatical forms — a `fullmatch`, nothing left over. An unrecognised construction does not match, and an unmatched segment refuses. The gap in my grammar is a dropped true sentence; the gap in theirs was a false claim reaching the operator's record. I was careful to state the property narrowly in the docstring, because the previous version's grand claim had to be retracted: this does not say adding words can only move a text toward refusal. It says the excuse requires a positive full-consumption match, so unrecognised input cannot reach *accepted* through that path at all. That is smaller and it is true.

The part I did not expect was what measuring it turned up. The Lead Architect had ratified a ceiling that morning — false suppression must stay at or under 5%, against a live measured 2.94% — and he pushed back on my gate specifically to say the cheap error is the one this ticket exists to reduce, so do not treat it as free. He was right in a way that nearly bit me. Closing the "successfully completed" word-order hole means widening the claim pattern, and the obvious way to widen it is to mirror the verb list on both sides of the adverb. I measured that against the real shadow journal instead of assuming: mirroring `merged` along with the rest takes false suppression from 2.94% to **44.12%**, because twelve of the thirty-four live cycles say "all features were successfully merged", which is *true* of a failed run and which a golden case has pinned as acceptable since the guard was written. One verb, fifteen times the cost. Excluding it closes the same holes at 2.94%, and the finished design lands at **0.00%** — the live false suppression is bought back and all three correct catches are kept.

And underneath all of it, the thing nobody was looking for. While reconstructing the reviewer's attack set I ran it against *main*, not just the rejected branch, and six strings came back accepted. One was a known gap. The other five were a word-order inversion: the pattern required the verb before the adverb, so `successfully completed` matched nothing at all. Fifty-six of fifty-six adverb-first inversions were accepted on a failed run, against zero of twenty-four verb-first controls. A total blind spot, live in shipped code, in a guard whose entire job is to catch that sentence — and it is the *same* inversion as the miss that was caught live on 22 July and locked as a golden case. The lesson caught one pattern and never swept its siblings. This ticket had been framed for a day as "the guard refuses too much"; it was also refusing too little, and no one had asked because the false-suppression complaint was louder.

The trade I took and want on the record: qualified forms with a trailing adjunct — *"no acceptance tests passed after the wave gate failure"* — still refuse, because a trailing adjunct is exactly how the presupposition attack works (*"…until the retry"*), and no closed test separates a harmless tail from a polarity-flipping one. I could have bought those back by allowing trailing phrases and would have reopened the class that killed the fourth design. Refusing both is the fail-closed choice; it is locked by a test that names it as a priced residual so it stays visible instead of becoming folklore.

Proposed lesson (unnumbered, for the integrator): *when a screen's accept side is defined by the absence of a marker, every gap in your knowledge becomes a false accept; define the accept side by a positive, total match instead and the same gaps become false refusals. Prefer the design whose unknown-unknowns land on the cheap side.*

Second proposed lesson: *a fix that closes one instance of a class must be swept against its siblings before the incident is called closed — the 22 July word-order miss was locked as a golden case while the identical inversion sat open in the same pattern for another day.*
