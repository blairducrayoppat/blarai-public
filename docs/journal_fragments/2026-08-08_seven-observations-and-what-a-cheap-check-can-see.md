### 2026-08-08 — Seven observations, and the line between what a cheap check can see and what it cannot

*Plain summary: he made seven observations about a website in about a minute. I went through them one at a time and asked which could be checked by a program that never opens an image and never runs the site. Four could. The instruments now say so — and, more importantly, they say out loud which three they cannot.*

The temptation after his review was to build one big grader. What actually helped was taking his seven observations literally and sorting them by what would be needed to catch each one.

Two were pure filesystem questions. *"View Details leads to a JSON error"* — does the file exist? *"The same images were used over and over"* — how many distinct pictures do six products point at? Neither needs a model, a browser, or a pixel. Both are now checked in about a tenth of a second, and both fire on the real delivery.

One was almost checkable and turned out to be the interesting one. *"Descriptions of the products don't match the image."* You cannot compare a description to a photograph without looking at the photograph. But you can notice that three bowls are rendering a file called `hero.png` — and the reason that convicts is not a subject comparison at all. `hero.png` names no subject; nothing about the filename says *not a bowl*. What convicts it is that the asset is page **furniture**, and a product is wearing it. That is a different question from "does this picture show a bowl", and it happens to be answerable.

Four remain out of reach: whether a picture is any good, whether the prose is any good, whether the layout holds at phone width, whether the whole thing feels like a shop. Those are his.

The design decision I spent longest on was the repeat threshold, and it is worth writing down because the reasoning generalises. Two products sharing a photograph is a coin flip — colour variants, a boxed pair, one shot of a series. Three is a *pattern*, and a pattern in generated content is a pipeline defect rather than a coincidence: the generator produced fewer subjects than the data declares and the placer reused what it had. Setting it at two would have bought recall at the cost of precision, and a gate that nags a coder about correct markup stops being read. But the more important call was refusing to solve the logo problem with a threshold at all. A number tuned high enough to ignore a logo appearing on five pages would rot the first time somebody sold six of something. Instead the rule counts only assets an *item declares in the data source*, so page furniture is out of scope by construction. A structural exclusion cannot be undone by the next site's shape; a tuned one can.

The same instinct applied to the honesty rendering, which is now the part of this work I would defend hardest. Every one of these checks has four states on his page — found something, found nothing, could not run, was not run — and the last two say *unknown* rather than *fine*. Even the clean rendering states its own limit: *this check never opens an image*. That sentence costs nothing and buys the only thing that matters, which is that when a check says clean he can believe exactly what it says and no more. A page that let him read "no picture problems" as "the pictures are right" would be trading his trust for a nicer-looking report, which is the same currency this project has been overspending all week.

The gate caught me twice today, which is the correct number of times for a day with this much output. The first: a status line I wrote into the Definition of Done reading `Status: STARTED`, invented because it described how the evidence felt. STARTED is not in that document's legend, the grammar gate rejected it, and `PARTIAL` was the honest word anyway — one reviewed delivery is a data point, not the published rate the row asks for. A status invented to fit the evidence is a claim escaping the vocabulary that makes it checkable, which is precisely the defect class I spent the day building instruments against, aimed at the document rather than at a grader. The second: the size budget, breached by 284 bytes, because I had written today's arcs into the snapshot as though they were still news. They merged. They are history, and history lives in the journal and the tickets. What a successor needs from #1342 is not the story — it is the open half and tonight's observable.

Both subagents found real defects in their own first passes and said so unprompted. One discovered that grading a merged item list would report a false repeat on any repo shipping its catalogue twice, a shape these scaffolds actually produce. The other found that the tick character in a real skip message renders as mojibake in the console window he actually reads, because `cmd.exe` opens a `.cmd` in the OEM codepage. Neither would have been visible without running the real thing against the real artifact, and neither was in anybody's design.
